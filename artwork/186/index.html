EL apocalipsis se parece a un camping.

2025, Watercolor on paper, 29,7 x 21 cm.

The Long, Hot Afternoon at the End of the World

To paint a great event is a straightforward commission. History painting has a deep vocabulary for the coronation, the battle, the decisive moment, the signing of the treaty. To paint the day after is a harder task. A painter who wants to show us not the catastrophe but its aftermath must find a language for the quiet, the empty, the exhausted. The real challenge is to paint the boredom of survival without making a boring painting. Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s small watercolor, EL apocalipsis se parece a un camping (The apocalypse looks like a campsite), meets that problem with a title that is also a thesis, deflating the grandest of all narratives into a scene of tacky, uncomfortable leisure.

The work’s argument is complete before you have even properly looked at it. The apocalypse, it suggests, is not the sublime terror of a John Martin painting, all fire and wrathful skies. The apocalypse on offer is a provisional, slightly disappointing affair, like a weekend spent in a field with bad plumbing and not enough to do. The thinness of the watercolor itself contributes to the feeling of an event that has lost its substance. The bruised peach of the sky bleeds into a sickly yellow horizon, the pigments staining the paper rather than building a solid world on top of it. The effect is a landscape where things have been taken away. What is left is a residue, a world washed out and waiting for nothing in particular.

The human figures, such as they are, seem to have suffered the same dilution. Rather than survivors huddled in the ruins, the figures are pink, fleshy pools of indolence. Rendered in a vibrant, chemical rosé, they seem to be either dissolving into the turf or failing to fully coalesce out of it, their forms blurring into the indistinct masses that have populated his recent paintings. The depiction of strange, vacant modern leisure represents an idea he has been approaching for years, seen in the uneasy gathering of Siesta en el camping from 2019, or the more frantic, crowded scene of El fin del mundo from a decade and a half before. Here, the idea reaches its terminal state. The figures are not people anymore; they are the abstract shape of lassitude itself. The risk, and it is a real one, is that this solution is too neat, a conceptual pun where the figures become literal puddles. The bodies threaten to become merely decorative, a splash of colour in a desolate landscape.

Yet the scene is saved from abstraction by its scattered, stubborn details. The animals grazing in the distance, indifferent. A wrecked car, slumped into the earth. Whether the figure pointing towards the horizon is making a discovery or performing an action emptied of all purpose is a distinction the painting refuses to make. These figures share a space, but they do not appear to share a reality, a condition that has haunted Moreno Sánchez’s work since early canvases like Transeúntes. A strange ancestor for the painting can be found in the bleak landscapes of the American West after the great bison herds were annihilated in the nineteenth century, rather than in the history of apocalyptic art. Those pictures also depicted an end of a world, leaving behind a silent, bleached-out terrain dotted with the evidence of what was lost.

What finally holds the eye is the car. Like the figures, it is a piece of tired matter. It has not crashed in any dramatic fashion; it has given up. Its metallic shell has the same weary, sun-baked quality as the ground it is sinking into. The car is no longer a symbol of mobility or escape, but another piece of exhausted hardware in a world that has run out of road, out of energy, out of ideas. The car is the perfect emblem for an apocalypse whose primary feature has replaced terror with a profound and unshakable fatigue.
