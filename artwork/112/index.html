Verano luminoso I.

2012, Watercolor on paper, 11,5 x 10,5 cm.

The Beauty of the Unpainted Mark

A painting that knows it is a watercolor is a rare thing. So much of the medium’s history is a story of resistance, of the fight to tame its fluid nature, to make it behave like oil paint, to force its bleeding edges into hard lines and its transparent washes into opaque solids. The test of skill was control. A great watercolor was one that disguised its own liquid origins. But a different path has always been available: to embrace the medium, to let the water do the work of making the world, and to accept that the world it makes will be one of perpetual dissolution.

This is the path Juanma Moreno Sánchez takes in Verano luminoso I. The painting is an act of surrender. The work is a picture of water made with water, and the result is a scene where everything is coming apart. The horizon is not a line but a tide mark, the high point of a wash of deep blue. Below it, the distinction between seafoam, light, and flesh dissolves. A figure, or the memory of one, lounges in the surf, but its body is just a stain of brown pigment bleeding into the lighter blue of the shallows. The boundary is not an outline but a chemical negotiation, a place where one color seeps into another. This effect is not a failure of control but an honest depiction of the subject. To be in the summer sea is to feel the edges of the self blur. In this small work, and in others from the same 2012 series like Verano luminoso II, Moreno Sánchez makes that feeling visible.

The most powerful element in the composition, however, is the one he did not paint. The fierce brightness of the scene, the glare of a high summer sun, is nothing more than the bare, white paper. In traditional watercolor, leaving the paper untouched is the primary way to represent white. But here, the function is more profound. The paper is not just a highlight; it is the active source of the picture’s energy, the blinding light into which the forms are dissolving. This feels close to the Japanese aesthetic principle of yohaku no bi, the beauty of empty space, where the unpainted part of an image is a vital and active component. The void is not a void; it is the sky, the mist, the light. Here, the whiteness is the very heat of the luminous summer named in the title, an emptiness that carries all the painting's weight.

Compared to the dense, architectural figures in his earlier oils like Piso de estudiantes, this body is a ghost. The figure is a presence defined by its own lack of substance. Yet the work is not entirely comfortable in its own ephemerality. The second form, floating to the left of the main figure, is a point of deep ambiguity. The form is a shape I cannot name with any confidence: a stray limb, a piece of driftwood, a shadow. The painting seems unsure what it is, and this indecision is its most vulnerable, and therefore most critical, moment. In a work so committed to dissolution, this single form holds back, a stubborn piece of matter that has not quite surrendered to the light.

In 2012, the year this watercolor was made, Moreno Sánchez was also producing large, complex oil paintings full of narrative tension, like the unsettling diptych El silencio (I). These small, quick studies of water and light are their opposite: sensations, not stories. They feel like a parallel practice, a quiet and private inquiry happening alongside the major works. They are paintings about the pleasure of watching a pool of pigment dry on a sheet of paper, of seeing a world form and un-form in a matter of minutes. The final image is less a picture of a moment than the residue of one, the faint stain a fleeting summer joy leaves behind.
