Voyeur.

2010, Watercolor on paper, 30,5 x 45,5 cm.

The Nearness of Strangers

The blank paper in a watercolor is never an empty space. That emptiness is a decision. The paper is the source of all light, the ground against which every color is read, and leaving it bare is the most deliberate thing a painter can do. The white can be a glare, a silence, or a refusal to speak. Sometimes, it is the distance between one mind and another, made visible.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Voyeur is a painting about two worlds that fail to touch. On one side, a knot of young people are tangled together on picnic blankets, a single organism of shared attention. Their limbs overlap, their heads are inclined towards a common center. Moreno renders them as a single passage of damp, bleeding color, their individual forms dissolving into the group. On the other side, a man in a straw hat walks alone. He is painted with a drier brush, his form more contained. He and the group are only a few feet apart on the same patch of grass, but the wide, unpainted gulf of paper that separates them makes the distance feel absolute. They are neighbors in space, but aliens to each other’s reality. This theme of social isolation marks a recurring preoccupation in Moreno’s work from this period; paintings like "Piso de estudiantes" and "Domingo por la tarde en el parque" are also fascinated by the way groups can form these self-contained islands of feeling.

The title accuses someone. A voyeur is a person who looks when they should not. The obvious suspect is the man with the metal detector, whose strange hobby feels like an intrusion into the pastoral scene. But he is not looking at the group. His posture is entirely given over to his task, his gaze directed downward by the machine he holds, a tool for looking for things no one else can see. He is searching for lost coins and forgotten metal, for the tiny, discarded history just beneath the surface. His is an active, obsessive looking, while the group seems absorbed in a state of being, not seeing. The painting proposes two different ways of being in the world, and finds them mutually invisible.

This composition of overlapping solitudes finds a distant echo in Japanese ukiyo-e prints from the Edo period. In works by artists like Kitagawa Utamaro, figures often share a teahouse or a landscape but remain locked in their own private gestures and thoughts. This was not necessarily a vision of modern alienation, but a depiction of a social world understood as a collection of concurrent, self-possessed interior lives. Moreno’s watercolor, though born of a different time and place, arrives at a similar place, using the quick, suggestive language of the medium to capture the psychological spaces that open up between people, even in the closest proximity.

If the man is not the voyeur, then who is? The act of looking that feels most like a trespass is the painter’s own. Moreno made a whole series of works around this time, titled "Stolen pic I", "Stolen pic II" and "Stolen pic III", that confess to this kind of theft. Watercolor, with its demand for speed, is the perfect medium for a thief. It snatches a moment, getting down the gist of a scene before it changes or notices it is being watched. The painting is the evidence of the voyeurism, the captured glance.

The picture is not perfect. The man with the metal detector risks becoming a type, a stock character representing a lonely pastime, and his presence feels almost too neat as a counterpoint to the youthful group. And I cannot be sure what to make of the large, abstract wash of purplish-brown that looms behind the picnickers. The shape is not quite a shadow and not quite a bush. The mark is a shape of pure paint, a beautiful, unexplained mark that sits in the scene without belonging to it.

Perhaps the key is the pair of white flip-flops, lying abandoned by the blankets. They are the one perfectly legible pair in the painting, an image of simple, untroubled connection at rest, while the humans around them are either tangled up or miles away in their heads.
