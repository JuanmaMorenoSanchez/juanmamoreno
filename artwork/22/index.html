Madonna (II).

2020, Oil on canvas, 147 x 116 cm.

The Body in the Wrong Garden

A body born from code has no natural habitat. It arrives with an anatomy but no environment, a form but no world to which that form is native. This presents a problem for a painter. To leave the figure suspended in a black, iconic void is one solution, turning it into a specimen. But to place it in a landscape is to make a much stranger claim: that this creature belongs on earth, under a sky, among plants. Such a placement asserts a right to place, and in the tension of that claim, a painting finds its argument. What does a world look like that could contain such a being?

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Madonna (II) takes the synthetic, alien deity first proposed in Madonna (I) and gives it a location. The first painting was an icon, a shimmering, unreadable, golden form presented against a reverent darkness, like an object for a cult that did not yet exist. This second work is a confrontation. The being has landed. It sits under a brilliant, cloudless, and unnervingly even blue sky, a sky that feels less like atmosphere and more like a digital backdrop. The question is no longer simply what this thing is, but where it is, and the answer seems to be: nowhere it recognizes. The few green plants that surround it are themselves simplified, a painter’s shorthand for nature rather than nature itself.

This gesture runs directly counter to a long tradition of placing holy figures in the world. For a painter like the 16th-century master Joachim Patinir, the inventor of the world landscape, the figure was often a small anchor for a vast, sprawling, and minutely detailed vision of creation. In his Landscape with St. Jerome, the saint is a tiny incident in God’s vast and glorious creation; the landscape is the true subject. Moreno Sánchez stages the exact opposite. The figure is monumental, a chaotic pile-up of biomorphic, mechanical, and textile parts that consumes the frame. The landscape is the incident, a barely-sketched context that offers no comfort and no home. The relationship between the being and its world is broken. The result is not harmony; it is a category error.

The assembly of a scene from parts that do not belong to one another is a persistent concern in his work. Canvases like El silencio (I), with its figures occupying the same frame but different seasons, show an early interest in staging these quiet, impossible collisions. The strangeness here, however, comes from a confrontation not between two human realities but between the artificial and a world that is supposed to be natural. This confrontation marks a profound shift from an earlier, more romantic engagement with place, such as the remembered, hazy light of Paisaje nostálgico. In Madonna (II), both the figure and the landscape it inhabits feel equally synthetic, products of different systems that have been forced into an unhappy cohabitation.

The painting’s success depends on this tension, but one detail threatens to resolve it too easily. Low in the composition, a passage of grey fabric seems to depict the pocket and waistband of a pair of jeans, a clear and legible sign of the human world. This passage of fabric is the only part of the sprawling form that feels familiar. In a being composed of molten gold, flowing drapery, and what might be crimson lobster shell, this fragment of the mundane feels like an over-correction, a gesture of reassurance the painting may not need. It attempts to anchor the alien form in our world, but the result is a dissonance that feels less profound than the others. Whether it is a moment of deliberate awkwardness or a seam showing in the construction of the impossible, I cannot be sure.

What remains is a monument to dislocation. And at the bottom right, almost too small to notice, are two tiny, simplified figures, looking up. They are rendered in the same palette as the main form, as if they were chips off the old block. Are they worshippers? Offspring? Or parasites feeding on the larger being? The painting provides no answer. It leaves us with a new god stranded in the wrong garden, attended by dependents whose relationship to it remains the deepest mystery of all.
