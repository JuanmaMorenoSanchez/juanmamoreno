Asunción.

2015, Oil on canvas, 195 x 130 cm.

The Assumption of Everybody

Painting a miracle now means staging an argument. In a world stripped of a shared cosmology, a painter who reaches for a subject like a body ascending to heaven cannot simply repeat the swirling figures and golden light of the Counter-Reformation. To do so would be an act of nostalgia, a retreat into a language few still speak with a straight face. The honest work, and the harder work, is to take that old story and force it into a collision with the world we actually have: a world of cheap clothes and jarring signals, of messy identities and a profound confusion about what, if anything, is sacred. The miracle, if it happens, happens in the friction.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Asunción (Assumption) is a painting built from that friction. It borrows the armature of a sacred scene (a body rising, arms outstretched in triumph, bathed in a halo of fire) and dresses it in the most profane costume imaginable. The ascending figure wears not flowing robes but a striped dress and a denim jacket, her feet shod in cheap flip-flops. Below her, where the apostles would traditionally gaze up in wonder, two men in swim trunks splash in murky water, their gestures caught between awe and horseplay. The painting’s title makes a holy promise that the canvas immediately breaks, staging a glorious ascension on a patch of grubby water surrounded by seagulls.

The greatest point of impact is the face. The face is a performance, a mask of sorts, bearded and moustached, that refuses to resolve into a stable identity. Presenting this face is the painting’s most confrontational act. It denies us the serene, beatific face of a saint and offers instead a joyful, deliberate artifice. The work finds a predecessor in Moreno Sánchez’s own exhausted gesture from the previous year, Otro puto selfie (“Another fucking selfie”), but where that painting sighed at the problem of self-representation, this one offers a loud, chaotic solution. The liberation here is not spiritual but social; it is the ecstatic release that comes from assuming a new identity, from the performance of a self that gleefully refuses to add up.

This is a very different kind of ecstasy from the one that animated the Spanish Baroque painters with whom the work argues. In Jusepe de Ribera’s charged, earthy painting of The Assumption of Mary Magdalene, a body is also pulled upwards, but her ascent is a mixture of agony and release, a soul straining to escape the confines of flesh. Ribera painted the desperate escape from the body; Moreno Sánchez paints a body celebrating its own impossible construction. Where Ribera’s Magdalene is one unified being in the grip of a divine force, Moreno Sánchez’s figure is a collage of clashing signals, a being held together by the sheer force of its own joyful will.

Painted in 2015, Asunción marks a turn. It carries the allegorical weight of earlier, more somber works like La búsqueda del dios (2011), but channels it into a new, explosive register that anticipates the costumed figures and uncanny bodies of pictures like Easter bunny. The painting is a hinge point, a canvas where a long-held set of questions about faith and meaning crash into the riotous, messy vernacular of the present moment.

The painting takes a significant risk. The pile-up of visual noise (the sacred pose, the contemporary clothes, the theatrical face, the apocalyptic light) threatens to collapse into a spectacular but incoherent gesture. Some might argue that the solution is too quick, that the painted moustache is a theatrical flourish that doesn't entirely earn its disruptive power. I am not certain the painting survives the gamble. But it stands as a loud, complicated and deeply honest portrait of a very modern kind of transcendence: the belief that you can be saved not by being lifted out of this world, but by learning to assemble yourself a new one from its broken pieces.
