Monstruo del espacio latente (IV).

2020, Oil on wood, 25 x 25 cm.

The Creature Without a Key

To paint a hybrid was once to deliver a message. For centuries, a creature that fused the parts of one animal with another, or with a human, arrived with its meaning attached. A sphinx was a riddle, a centaur was a war between reason and instinct, an angel was a vessel for the divine. The form was the story, and its audience was expected to know how to read it. The most elaborate of these systems was the medieval bestiary, where a lion-headed man was an emblem of some virtue or vice, a legible piece of a moral code, instead of simply a marvel. A painter who now creates such a being, however, begins with a new problem: what is a monster for, once you have thrown away the book that explains it?

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Monstruo del espacio latente (IV) stages a quiet, impossible encounter that refuses any easy decoding. The title itself is a confession of origins. This creature comes from the “latent space,” the conceptual void from which a generative algorithm conjures its images, rather than from myth or scripture. The painting is a translation, rendering a machine’s dream in the slow, resistant medium of oil on wood. Here, a figure with the body of a man and the soft, sad head of a lion gazes at a companion. This second figure is huddled under a white shroud, a form defined only by its own withdrawal. The scene is static, a moment of watching. This painting is the calmest of its series; a canvas like Monstruo del espacio latente (I) from the same year is a storm of convulsive, solitary flesh. The painting's subject is the relationship between two, instead of the monster.

A work like the thirteenth-century illustration of a manticore in the Bodleian Library’s MS. Bodley 764 offers a useful contrast. That creature, with its lion’s body and human head, was an entry in a visual encyclopedia of meaning; its strangeness served a clear theological purpose. Moreno Sánchez borrows the grammar of the hybrid but discards the dictionary. His lion-man carries no symbolic weight. Its meaning is emotional rather than allegorical, located entirely in the gentleness of its gaze. The pathos of the scene comes precisely from the fact that these beings have no story, no world, and no assigned place. They are monsters of circumstance, born of a process and given a brief, tender life on a small wooden square.

This use of the non-human face as an emotional register runs through his work, but to different ends. A decade earlier, in Hijos de puta, the animal was a cheap mask worn by men in suits, a tool of savage caricature that reduced power to its most brutish form. In this painting from 2020, the animal head is not a mask but a face, capable of a sorrow and concern that feels more human than monstrous. The scene’s intimacy finds a parallel in other works from the same year, like the sleeping figures in Cruzando la meseta, who find a fragile shelter in each other. But the introduction of the non-human here changes the terms of tenderness, making it stranger and more precarious.

The painting’s greatest risk is its own poignancy. The quiet sadness of the encounter, the vulnerability of the huddled form, and the almost compassionate expression of the lion-man all conspire to make the creature sympathetic. It is easy to read the painting as a simple metaphor for loneliness, for the gentle monster ostracized by the world. I am not certain the painting entirely escapes being domesticated by this interpretation, its profound strangeness neutralized into a touching fable. The loose, energetic brushwork and the small scale both suggest a fleeting, private feeling rather than a durable, mythic presence. Perhaps that is the point.

The entire canvas hinges on that one-way connection, the look that passes from the monster to the figure who will not, or cannot, look back. We are made to share the gaze of the creature, watching over something that seems to be hurt, or lost, or simply asleep. The flecks of orange that litter the blue ground could be dying embers or the last lights of a city seen from a great distance; the painting gives us no way to know. What is left is not a story or a symbol, but the plain fact of the vigil itself. It is a portrait of care, offered by a creature with no name to a body with no face, in a world that does not exist.
