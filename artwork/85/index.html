Jardín triturado.

2022, Oil on canvas, 190 x 190 cm.

The World Landscape After the Accident

To hold more than one time in a single frame has always been a problem for painting. One solution, invented in the sixteenth century for the delight of wealthy armchair travelers, was the world landscape. A painter like Joachim Patinir, its chief pioneer, would construct a vast, imaginary panorama seen from an impossible height, a composite of every kind of geography, and then populate it with several different biblical stories at once. The trick was coherence. The light was consistent, the high horizon held, and the world, for all its busyness, felt like a single, legible creation seen from a god’s-eye view. Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Jardín triturado begins by walking into that same ambition and then stages its violent collapse. The painting is a world landscape that has been put through a shredder. The title says as much. The painting is not a view, but the wreckage of one.

The shredding is a deliberate method, an act of controlled sabotage that appears elsewhere in his work. A canvas from two years prior, Partido de beisbol triturado (2020), applies the same logic to a different kind of public space. Here, in the garden, the effect is one of narrative catastrophe. A playground slide, rendered in a gleaming, inorganic blue, has swollen to the size of a brutalist monument and crashed into the field. A horse rears in the middle distance, its rider a smear of white paint, as if it belongs to an entirely different historical genre. The scale is broken. The light shifts from a hazy, nostalgic gold on the figures to a flat, indifferent blue in the sky. The space refuses to resolve into a single, believable place. The composition is a collage of moments, of atmospheres, of painterly languages, all forced to occupy the same square of canvas.

This anxiety is a long way from where Moreno Sánchez’s paintings of leisure began. A small watercolour from 2009, Domingo por la tarde en el parque, shows a world of simple, tumbling joy, its figures dissolving into cartwheels of pure motion. The park was once a space of unselfconscious freedom. By 2022, it has become a problem. The garden is now the site of a memory that will not hold still, a place of play so thoroughly fragmented that it feels more like the scene of an accident. The innocence of the earlier work has curdled into a deep and unsettling ambiguity, a sense that the past is a place you can see but never re-enter on its own terms.

The figures are not characters in a story; they are specimens dropped in from other realities. The two children in the foreground, with their obscured faces and oddly formal postures, seem to have wandered in from a faded photograph. The woman to their right, however, is painted with a startling clarity. She is the picture’s anchor and its most significant risk. Rendered with a contemporary, almost casual realism, she threatens to domesticate the chaos around her, to pull the whole impossible scene back into the orbit of a normal afternoon. That she fails to do so is the point. Her solid presence makes the fragmentation of the world behind her feel all the more violent. She is the stable object against which the catastrophe is measured.

What, then, of the great blue form that dominates the center? This central form is the engine of the painting’s collapse. The object is a slide, but also a claw, a piece of industrial machinery, a sculpture. Its identity remains radically uncertain. The blue form is a piece of the world that has forgotten its own name, rendered in a slick, hard-edged style that feels alien to the soft grass it has torn apart. This monstrous object is where the logic of the scene finally and completely breaks down. It feels like an import from the synthetic, inhuman spaces that would come to define his later paintings of monstrous bodies, like those in the Monstruo del espacio latente series. The object is a premonition, an ambassador from a nature that is not natural at all.

And yet, scattered across the grass are oranges. They are the simplest, most ordinary things in the frame. They are whole, round, and profoundly real. In a garden that has been shredded, a landscape of broken parts and colliding worlds, they are the only things that seem to have survived the machine. They lie in the foreground, small, solid facts in a world that has otherwise come undone. They offer no story and no comfort. They are just there, having rolled clear of the wreckage.
