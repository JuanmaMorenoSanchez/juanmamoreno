Festival de influencers.

2025, Oil on canvas, 100 x 120 cm.

The Glitch in the Garden

To paint a crowd used to be to paint a single body, a collective organism moved by one purpose: the burghers of a Dutch city, the peasants at a Flemish wedding, the soldiers in a history painting. The crowd shared a world. A painter who attempts the subject now faces a new problem. The modern crowd is often just an aggregation of solitudes, a gathering of people who share a space but not a reality. Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Festival de influencers begins here, by painting a festival where no one seems to be at the same party.

The title names a celebration, but the canvas shows us a series of private worlds colliding. A figure in a pink costume, part bird, part pyjama suit, stands with its back to us, a recurring motif of performance and anonymity in Moreno Sánchez’s work that appeared years earlier in El artista excéntrico. Another figure on the right is fragmented, her body dissolving into a thicket of predatory-looking plants as she holds a cigarette. The figures are not participants in a shared event; they are adjacent realities, held together only by the artist’s frame. This theme of the disconnected gathering has a history in his work, but where a painting like Pantallas showed an aftermath, a world of debris, this 2025 canvas depicts the performance in full swing. The alienation is not a consequence; it is the main event.

The painting’s structure is an aggressive collage, a technique that has been used for over a century to import fragments of the real world onto the canvas. But where the Cubists pasted newsprint to break up the picture plane, Moreno Sánchez paints the logic of the digital feed directly. The hard-edged block of sky behind the pink figure looks imported from another image entirely, a separate window layered on top of the scene. The luridly perfect oranges seem less like fruit and more like sponsored content. This finds a strange and distant ancestor in the work of James Rosenquist, who took the visual grammar of billboard advertising and used it to build his monumental Pop paintings. A work like President Elect forced together a politician’s face, a car, and a slice of cake to expose the machinery of American desire. Moreno Sánchez performs a similar operation on the visual language of influence, showing us a world built from incompatible, professionally optimistic fragments.

At the heart of the festival are two glowing, blank screens. They are the brightest things in the painting, sources of a sterile, sourceless light. Unlike the iPad in a previous painting that illuminated a face, these screens illuminate nothing. They are active voids, platforms waiting for content, their blankness a kind of humming, expectant silence. Seeing them as an easy symbol for technological emptiness is tempting, and perhaps they are. Yet their sheer objecthood, the thickness of the white paint laid on to form their surfaces, makes them feel less like symbols and more like dumb, physical facts. They are the altars at the centre of this strange ceremony, and it is not clear whether anyone is meant to be worshipping.

The real argument of the canvas happens in the paint itself. The flat, pasted-on quality of the sky fights with the visceral, churning vegetation that seems to be consuming the woman on the right. This tension between the slick surface of the image and the messy reality underneath it is a central concern of Moreno Sánchez’s recent work, which has consistently given fleshy, uncomfortable weight to digital phantoms and hybrid bodies, as in the unsettling figures of Catástrofe en el bosque. The painting’s title may be satirical, but the act of painting is not. The canvas insists on the material reality of this fractured world, rendering its absurdities with a straight face. In the end, the most telling detail might be the smallest. Amidst the costumes, the screens, and the performance, the woman on the right lifts a cigarette to her lips. The act is a quiet, unglamorous, and deeply human gesture, a small crack of private habit in the seamless facade of the festival.
