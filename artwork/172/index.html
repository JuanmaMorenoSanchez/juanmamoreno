Crucifijo.

2011, Oil on wood, 30 x 40 cm.

The Unattended Stage

A painter who places a crucifix at the center of a work today is not making a religious statement but asking a question about a piece of furniture. The crucifix, emptied of its universally binding power, has become an object, a shape with a history, something to be arranged in a room. Or, in this case, in a strange and dusty yard under a deep blue sky. The problem for the painter is no longer how to illustrate a sacred story, but what to do with the props after the story has ended and the audience has gone home.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Crucifijo from 2011 is a catalogue of these abandoned props. The central crucifix is not an object of veneration but an item of outdoor statuary, no more or less important than the orange tree beside it, which has a lit chandelier tangled in its branches. A formal, cloth-draped table stands before the cross, set not for a sacrament but with an array of indistinct white shapes, like a shrine to something that has been forgotten. Nearby, another table stands ready for a party that is not happening, and in the foreground, a pink, overstuffed armchair waits for a guest who will never arrive. The painting is a stage set for a series of events that refuse to coalesce into a single narrative. This method of assembling incompatible realities in one frame is a hallmark of his work from this period, seen in the temporal collision of Los carcamales que seremos and the allegorical pile-up of La búsqueda del dios, both also from 2011.

The scene’s closest spiritual ancestor is not in the history of Spanish religious painting, but in the metaphysical cityscapes of Giorgio de Chirico. In paintings like The Soothsayer's Recompense, de Chirico created a similar sense of desolate waiting by arranging classical statues, brick walls, and distant trains in impossible combinations under a harsh, raking light. The feeling is not of fantasy but of profound dislocation, the dream-logic of a world where objects have become detached from their meanings. Moreno Sánchez achieves a similar effect, but where de Chirico’s plazas were haunted by classical antiquity, this backyard is haunted by a more recent, Catholic past. The painting suggests that for a certain generation in Spain, the objects of faith now feel as distant and mysterious as a Roman statue.

This approach has its own risks. The accumulation of strange objects, the chandelier in the tree in particular, can feel like a self-conscious quirkiness, a solution taken too easily. The painting sometimes seems to be on the verge of becoming an inventory of surrealist gestures. I cannot be sure that it entirely escapes this. The work’s real power might reside in its quieter, less theatrical elements: the deep, empty blackness of the windows in the adjacent building, the open doorway that leads into a darkness as absolute as the one outside. Such elements mark the moments where the painting stops performing its strangeness and simply allows a deep and unsettling silence to enter the frame.

The light is the force that holds this collection of anxieties together. Its glow belongs to no time of day. It comes from the electric globes of the chandelier, from some unseen source off-stage that casts the long shadow of the cross, and from the lit interior of the building. This collection of sources produces a theatrical, inconsistent light that does not illuminate so much as it exposes everything as part of a deliberate construction. Such theatrical lighting is a recurring feature in the nocturnal scenes from this period, like Polvo en el cementerio. The light is not there to reveal a truth. The light is there to show us that we are looking at a painting, a carefully arranged set of propositions on a small wooden board, an empty stage where the meaning of every object is up for debate.
