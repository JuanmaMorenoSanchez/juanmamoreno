Verano luminoso V.

2012, Watercolor on paper, 10,5 x 15,5 cm.

The Conspiracy of Looking

To paint what someone else is looking at is to paint a space you cannot enter. The painter stands behind the painted figure, showing us a shoulder, the back of a head, and a world that belongs only to the gaze of the person depicted. We see them seeing, but we are shut out from the sight itself. The effect is a classic composition of distance. But Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s small watercolor from 2012, Verano luminoso V (Luminous Summer V), proposes something different. This work is not about a gaze that excludes us. The painting positions us so closely behind the two boys at the water’s edge that it feels less like observation and more like participation, a shared confidence. We are not watching them look; we are looking with them.

This work belongs to a period, around 2012, when Moreno Sánchez was making series of small, quick watercolors that feel like private thoughts next to his larger, more public oil paintings. While canvases like Transeúntes (2008) or Pantallas (2010) wrestled with the fractured, anonymous nature of life in the city, the Verano luminoso series is an exercise in intimacy. In these works, as in this one, the world shrinks to the scale of a body and the water it touches. The watercolor is a medium of speed and transparency, a way of taking a note. These paintings feel like such notes, a sustained meditation on a quiet, recurring moment of summer leisure, seen in close-up, as in the nearly identical scene of Verano luminoso II.

The history of painting children by the water is dominated by grander statements. Joaquín Sorolla, Spain’s great painter of light, turned the beaches of Valencia into a theatre of sun-drenched, athletic joy, his canvases filled with the energy of boys running through the surf. His work is a celebration of brilliant light and vigorous life. Moreno Sánchez’s small, quiet picture politely refuses this tradition of performance. His figures are not active; they are absorbed. Their backs are turned to us purely because their attention is entirely consumed by the water before them. The watercolor’s subject is the stillness of contemplation rather than the joy of play.

The painting’s power is in this focus. The world is reduced to three elements: two bodies, the pooled turquoise water, and the bright, unpainted paper that serves as the shore. The two boys, one sitting and one crouching, form a single unit of attention, their postures echoing each other. The water is not a setting but the object of their shared inquiry. The water is where their gaze, and therefore ours, is directed. Moreno Sánchez allows the watercolor to bleed and pool, to be itself, so that the medium depicting the water takes on the liquid properties of its subject. The scene depicts a moment of intense, almost silent communion between the figures and the element they are studying. A different kind of investigation from this period can be seen in his watercolor Splash.

Into this quiet scene of blues and browns, there is one chromatic shock: a splash of brilliant magenta and red floating in the water. It might be a piece of discarded clothing or a lost toy, but its form is ambiguous, its color utterly alien to the rest of the palette. I am not certain what it is meant to be, and its refusal to be named gives it a strange power. It looks like a wound. The shape is a puncture in the otherwise tranquil surface, a jolt that pulls the eye and holds it there. The painting risks leaning too heavily on this single note of discord to give the scene its charge. Yet, it works. The red object is the detail that prevents the watercolor from dissolving into simple nostalgia. The red object is the question mark that floats in the luminous summer, suggesting that even in moments of shared peace, there is always an object from another story, a stray fact that refuses to belong.
