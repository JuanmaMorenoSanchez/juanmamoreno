Carne.

2012, Oil on canvas, 130 x 97 cm.

The Landscape in the Lap

To paint an appetite is to choose a side. One can render the seductive gleam of the commodity, a path well-trod by Pop Art until the road gave out. One can document the abject mechanics of the supply chain, a project of moral accounting that often serves the painter’s conscience more than the painting. Or a painter can attempt something stranger: to build a world where the consumer and the consumed are forced to occupy the same impossible space, a world where the logic of the food chain collapses into a single, monstrous geography.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s painting from 2012 does exactly this. The title, Carne, names the subject three times over. The subject is the meat in the hamburger the woman lifts to her lips, the living flesh of the pigs that forage in the miniature landscape that sits in her lap, and her own body, which looms over the scene like a fleshy mountain range. The painting is not a sequence but a system, a closed and perfect circuit of consumption presented as a single, static fact. She is a giantess, a new kind of goddess for a world of disposable goods, and the classical landscape has been reduced to a diorama at her feet, a pastoral scene as a pedestal for a meal.

There is a long history of painters using scale to make an argument about humanity’s place in the world. The 16th-century Flemish master Joachim Patinir is often credited with inventing the world landscape, a genre of vast, panoramic views in which tiny human figures act out biblical or mythological dramas. In a painting like his Landscape with the Flight into Egypt, the scale delivers a clear theological lesson: the world is God’s, and humanity is a small incident within it. Moreno Sánchez takes this convention and turns it violently inside out. Here, the human is the vast, overwhelming feature. The appetite is the landscape, and the world is just a small, brown patch of earth that serves it.

This strategy of colliding two incompatible realities into one frame is a recurring feature of Moreno Sánchez’s work from this period. A year earlier, in Los carcamales que seremos, a quiet domestic interior gives way to a grim, prophetic pageant of old age unfolding outside. In the same year as Carne, the two paintings titled El silencio place figures dressed for different seasons and suffering different emergencies side-by-side in a shared, incoherent disaster. Carne uses the same device, but replaces the collision of times or climates with a collision of scale. The painting offers a more direct, almost diagrammatic version of the same thought, refining a fascination with appetite visible in earlier works like Comedor de gambas into a clear, mythological statement.

What stops the painting from being a simple sermon against consumption is the woman’s face. Her gaze is direct, aimed straight at us, and it is a look of unambiguous pleasure. She is not a monster of gluttony, but a person enjoying her food. The painting refuses to condemn the appetite it depicts, choosing instead to grant it the world-making power of a geological force. In this choice, the work takes its greatest risk. The allegory is so complete, the circuit between pig and burger and mouth so perfectly drawn, that it threatens to be almost too legible. Its success as a brutal, memorable image might leave little room for the doubt that animates so much of his other work. The result is a devastatingly effective statement, and I cannot quite decide if its clarity is a total victory or a solution reached a moment too soon.

The painting’s quietest, most unsettling detail may be the trees. The small cypresses that frame the miniature pig-sty lend the scene a classical, almost funereal dignity. They are borrowed from an older kind of landscape, one that spoke of permanence, history, and a stable relationship between humanity and the earth. Here, they are just decoration for a trough, a final, ironic nod to a world that has been entirely subsumed by the monumental, glorious, and terrible fact of a single body’s hunger.
