Asunción y descenso.

2020, Oil on wood, 200 x 200 cm.

The Four-Chambered Heart

To look at a single painting has always been to accept the terms of a single, coherent world. The four edges of the canvas are a contract: everything inside this frame belongs to one time, one place, one logic of light. To break that contract is to propose something else about how a life is lived. The single story gives way to a tiled screen of simultaneous, competing realities. A painting that offers four worlds at once is an argument that we no longer live in just one. It suggests our attention is already divided, our time already fractured, and that a more honest portrait of experience might be a grid.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Asunción y descenso is precisely such a grid. The title gives us the two primary verbs for a body in space: to rise and to fall. The canvas is a grammar of these actions, parsed across four panels that refuse to connect. A woman backflips in a moment of weightless grace against a twilight cityscape; another figure bends toward a fire in a field under a sunset’s orange glow; two more huddle by a campfire, lost in the small ritual of a toasted marshmallow; a tangle of legs tumbles into the hard blue geometry of a swimming pool. The painting is a direct argument with an earlier, simpler work of his, the 2015 Asunción, which showed one single, ecstatic figure rising. Five years later, the ascent is no longer solitary or straightforward. The ascent is twinned with its opposite and shattered into a constellation of moments that do not, and cannot, add up.

This form has a deep history. The small narrative panels of a Renaissance altarpiece predella, like the sequential scenes in Paolo Uccello’s The Miracle of the Desecrated Host, laid out a story step by step, with a clear moral direction. Moreno Sánchez borrows the tiled structure but sabotages the narrative engine. His panels are not sequential but simultaneous, a collage of leisure activities that share no timeline. One quadrant’s night is another’s day. The effect is a condition rather than a story. The painting is a portrait of a life lived in the perpetual present tense of the browser tab, where disparate windows of action and emotion sit side-by-side, each sealed in its own reality.

Within this structure, the painting quietly lays the groundwork for the monstrous forms that would come to define his later work. The jumble of limbs in the swimming pool quadrant, a confusion of foreshortened legs and brand-name sandals, is an anatomy of pure awkwardness. The jumble of limbs becomes a body made strange by gravity and a bad dive, rather than by an algorithm. This canvas from 2020 finds the uncanny in the utterly mundane, proving that Moreno Sánchez’s fascination with the impossible body, so visible in a later work like Ángel III, did not need a machine to get started. The raw material was already there in the clumsy, beautiful chaos of ordinary life, just as the disconnected social pods of his earlier crowd painting, Transeúntes, are here distilled into four separate cells.

The rigid geometry of the grid is both the work’s engine and its greatest risk. The hard black lines do so much of the work of separation that one might wonder if it is a solution taken too easily, enforcing a disconnection that a more integrated composition would have to earn. We cannot say whether we are seeing the same person across four different moments or four different people. The painting insists the question is irrelevant. The subject is not the identity of the figures, but the structure of their divided attention. They are avatars of ascent and descent, their individual stories sacrificed to the larger pattern.

What remains is the mind’s desire to bridge the gaps, a desire the painting’s structure constantly frustrates. We look for a narrative thread, a cause and effect that might link the campfire to the city or the backflip to the pool, but the cross at the heart of the painting holds them apart. The work’s final, and perhaps most resonant, subject is this imposed separation. The work is a painting about the impossibility of a single story, offering instead the four chambers of a modern heart, each beating to its own time.
