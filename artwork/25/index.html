Autorretrato con Puri (III).

2019, Oil on board, 40 x 30 cm.

A Portrait of Abdication

To look at a portrait has traditionally been to enter into a contract of gazes. The sitter looks out, the painter looks in, and we, arriving later, look at them both, trying to read the traces of that encounter. The entire genre is built on the premise of a face offered up for consideration, a self presented to be known. A painting that refuses this fundamental exchange, where every party averts their eyes, is not just avoiding a convention. The painting proposes that a portrait might be for something else entirely, something other than revelation.

This is a self-portrait about a quiet abdication. Juanma Moreno Sánchez gives the work his own name but denies us his face, bowing his head so completely that it becomes a dark, unreadable shape at the centre of the canvas. The title insists on a duet, Autorretrato con Puri (III), but the composition establishes a clear hierarchy. Puri, the cat, occupies the highest point in the picture, her body described with an alert, economical precision. The painter, by contrast, becomes a warm landscape, a living piece of topography for the cat to inhabit. In this series of portraits from 2019, including Autorretrato con Puri (I) and Autorretrato con Puri (IV), Moreno Sánchez repeatedly works through this same inversion: the artist painting himself as a willing subordinate, a creature defined by his utility to another. The self is not excavated, but offered up as a perch.

The act places the work in a lineage of profound, domestic quiet. The work shares a kinship here with the paintings of Gwen John, who filled her canvases with the silent, withdrawn life of women and cats in hushed Parisian rooms. In a work like her A Corner of the Artist's Room in Paris, the chair, the window, and the cat are given as much weight as any human presence. Her paintings create a world where being is more important than doing, a state of interiority rendered in chalky, close-valued tones. Moreno Sánchez’s painting shares that private frequency. Moreno Sánchez is not painting a personality, with its attendant drama and psychology. He is painting a relationship, describing it with the simple physics of weight, warmth, and balance rather than with expression.

The painting is made with a haste that feels like honesty. The oil is put on thinly, the weave of the board showing through, and an electric orange underpainting glows at the edges of the man’s shirt and the cat’s body. This is a moment captured, not a statement constructed. And yet, this very charm risks becoming an evasion. By turning away, by reducing his own presence to a dark mass holding a coffee cup, the painter sidesteps the harder questions of self-representation he had already acknowledged five years earlier in a work titled, with a sigh, Otro puto selfie. The solution here is gentle, affectionate, and perhaps a little too comfortable. The setting itself is uncertain; the bright turquoise sky suggests an exterior, but the intimate focus feels entirely domestic. The painting holds these contradictions lightly.

This moment of retreat into the personal stands as a quiet pivot in his work. These pictures from 2019 are a long way from the cool observation of anonymous crowds in paintings like Transeúntes from a decade before, and they feel like a different world from the unsettling, algorithm-born bodies that would emerge a few years later. It is a work of temporary peace, a willed submission. The portrait makes no grand claims. Its resolution is grounded in the small, material fact of a shared physical space, a brief alignment of needs, rather than in an idea about identity. The last word belongs to the small, brilliant blue of the mug, a point of ceramic certainty in a scene of fleeting, gentle life.
