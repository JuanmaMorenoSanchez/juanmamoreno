Mañaneo I.

2019, Oil on wood, 30 x 50 cm.

A Frieze for the Morning After

A group portrait is usually an argument for connection. Its subjects are bound by a meal, a conversation, a shared task, or a battle. Their gazes cross, their bodies turn towards one another, and the composition diagrams the social electricity between them. A harder problem, and perhaps a more modern one, is how to paint a group whose binding agent is a shared absence, a collective state of disconnection. How does a painter make a picture of a communion founded on collapse, where the only thing holding the figures together is the gravity that pins them to a single piece of furniture?

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Mañaneo I is a portrait of just such a group. The title, a Spanish colloquialism for the slow, hungover state of the morning after, gives us the context. The men are not resting in peaceful slumber. Theirs is the heavy, exhausted sleep of depletion. The three men are arranged across the canvas like figures on a classical frieze, a shallow relief of sleeping bodies. This formal arrangement lends an unlikely monumentality to their mundane exhaustion. Though they are not heroes fallen in battle, they are casualties nonetheless, their individual slumbers locking them into a shared, sculptural form.

They rest inside a colour, not a room. The wall behind them is a solid, uncompromising field of blue, so intense and flat it feels more like a digital screen than a painted surface. It has the depthless, backlit quality of a monitor’s error message, an artificial void that offers no comfort and no escape. The method is a recurring strategy for Moreno Sánchez, who uses colour not to describe a place but to establish a psychological climate. The blue here functions like an enclosure, sealing the figures off from the world and making their sleep feel less like rest and more like a form of quarantine. The light that falls on their limbs seems to come from nowhere, carving out the shapes of knees and arms with a clinical, detached clarity.

The arrangement of heavy, sleeping male bodies finds a distant but powerful echo in depictions of the sleeping apostles in the Garden of Gethsemane. In paintings like Andrea Mantegna’s panel from the 1450s, the apostles are not serene figures but lumpy, awkward bundles of flesh, overcome by a very human weakness at a moment of sacred crisis. Moreno Sánchez’s painting borrows that language of sculptural, undignified slumber and strips it of its religious narrative. What remains is the raw physicality of abandonment by one’s own energy instead of by a god. The moral failure of the apostles is replaced by the simple biological shutdown of the morning after. The painting does not mock its subjects; it finds a way to dignify their collapse by connecting it to one of painting’s old, serious forms.

This canvas is one of a pair, a theme he returned to in Mañaneo II, and it belongs to a long-running interest in the nature of rest, visible in other works like the sun-drenched Siesta en el camping. But it also speaks to his continuing study of the ways people share a space without connecting, a theme that runs from the public anonymity of Transeúntes to the private, singular worlds of these three sleepers. The painting is a group portrait defined by the solitude of each member, a vision of community as a temporary, unconscious huddle before life resumes.

If the painting has a fault, it is a small surrender to elegance. The central figure, with his crossed hands and perfectly tilted head, reclines with the grace of a classical river god on a cheap sofa. The graceful pose suggests a moment where the search for a pleasing form perhaps softens the raw, awkward mess of genuine exhaustion. The solution feels a fraction too composed. I cannot be sure if this was a deliberate choice or a painter’s instinct for order taking over. But the painting’s great strength is its refusal to tell a story or pass a judgment. We do not know what party led to this, and the painting insists that we do not need to. The anecdote is irrelevant. The state of being is everything.
