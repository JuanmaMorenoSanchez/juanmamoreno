Fiesta en el parking (III).

2018, Watercolor on paper, 29 x 21 cm.

To paint a moment of shared laughter is to risk a particular kind of failure. The subject is dangerously close to sentiment, to the simple sweetness of a greeting card. A painter who wants to depict that joy without cheapening it must find a way to complicate the story. The work has to build the scene and, in the same gesture, show you the precarious scaffolding that holds it up. The happiness must feel earned, which means it must also feel contingent, a temporary victory against a world that is not necessarily built for it.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s small watercolor, Fiesta en el parking (III), understands this perfectly. The title alone is a contract with a complex reality, grounding the tender, almost classical pose of the central figures (a man lifting a laughing woman) in the transient, graceless space of a car park. The darkness they emerge from is not the dramatic, velvet void of an old master portrait; it is the indifferent, unlit blackness between parked cars at night. The wash of watercolor that stands in for the man’s lower body bleeds into this darkness, dissolving him into the setting. The setting is not a stage for heroes. It is a party in a place you are not meant to linger.

This painting, from 2018, can be seen as a bright culmination of a certain kind of picture for Moreno Sánchez, one he would soon leave behind. The painting marks the last flash of uncomplicated human connection before his work turned, around 2020, toward the monstrous, algorithm-born figures of works like Monstruo del espacio latente (I). For several years, his canvases and papers were populated by the young and the restless at festivals, at street parties, in scenes of social awkwardness and chaotic fun, as in Noche caótica from the same year. This painting belongs to that world, but its mood is different. Where other pictures show alienation, this one offers a moment of pure, buoyant connection, a counterpoint to the bleaker version of the party seen in Fiesta en el parking (I). The painting is a portrait of a specific kind of modern Spanish nightlife, the world of the botellón, captured with an insider’s eye.

In this focus on the textures of ordinary urban life, the work finds a distant ancestor. The quick, descriptive line and focus on a social snapshot, rather than a grand narrative, connect it to the 19th-century lithographs of Honoré Daumier. Daumier was a relentless observer of Parisian society, capturing its citizens in railway carriages, bathhouses, and courtrooms with a vision that was at once satirical and deeply humane. Moreno Sánchez is doing something similar here, using the quick, direct medium of watercolor and marker to create a vignette of his own generation’s social rituals, finding in a provisional, messy gathering a moment worthy of a picture.

But the painting saves its most important argument for last, in a detail that is easy to miss. At the far right, a third hand enters the frame, gripping the hem of the woman’s striped dress. The gesture is supportive, a small act of stage management. And with that, the story changes completely. The scene is not a private, spontaneous moment between two people. It is a collective act, a performance for an audience. The woman’s joyous laugh is suddenly re-contextualized; perhaps it is aimed not at the man holding her, but at the friend just out of frame who is helping to hold this pose. The painting, it turns out, is a picture of people taking a picture. I cannot be certain whether the moment’s joy is genuine or performed for the camera that is implied to be there, and the painting is stronger for withholding that answer. Its only misstep, perhaps, is that this third hand is so subtle the entire critical mechanism might go unseen, leaving the work to be read as the very sentimental image it works so hard to subvert.

That single, disembodied hand is the key that unlocks the work. It transforms the painting from a simple portrait of happiness into a far more interesting and contemporary subject: the way happiness is constructed, documented, and performed within a group. It suggests that even our most intimate moments can be collaborations, staged and recorded for a future audience. The darkness of the car park is no longer just a backdrop. This darkness is the space that hides the rest of the party, the friends, and the phone that is almost certainly being held up to capture the instant, preserving a fragile moment of joy snatched from an unforgiving night.
