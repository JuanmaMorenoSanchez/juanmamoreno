Domingo por la tarde en el parque.

2009, Watercolor on paper, 30,5 x 45,5 cm.

An Argument on the Grass

A certain kind of modern painting approaches leisure with the solemnity of a state funeral. Georges Seurat’s pointillist monument to a Parisian Sunday, A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte, is the definitive document of this tendency. His park is a frozen, silent theatre of social positions, each figure locked into place by a rigid geometry of class and posture. The painting is a feat of control. The painting becomes, in its final effect, an image of a pleasure so structured it has ceased to look like pleasure at all. To take the same title, Domingo por la tarde en el parque, as Juanma Moreno Sánchez does for this watercolour from 2009, is to pick an argument with that ghost.

Moreno Sánchez’s reply is to turn the world upside down, literally. He replaces Seurat’s static procession with a tumble of bodies, a sequence of cartwheels that swaps monumental stillness for kinetic, clumsy joy. Where Seurat built his world from discrete, scientific dots of oil paint, Moreno Sánchez works in the wet, immediate bleed of watercolour. The green of the grass and a strange, bruised purple that might be a hedge or a shadow are allowed to pool and run into one another. The medium is not just a vehicle for the image; it is an active participant in the argument, its fluidity a direct contradiction of Seurat’s painstaking discipline. This watercolour is not a finished statement but a record of an event, as immediate and unrepeatable as the motion it describes. It shares a spirit with an earlier drawing like "After a bigger spash", which also finds its subject in a fleeting moment of bodies and water.

The work is a study not in anatomy, but in momentum. I am not sure if these are three different people or one person captured in three successive moments of a single, sprawling cartwheel, a sequence reminiscent of Eadweard Muybridge’s photographic studies of movement, like his 1887 plate of a woman turning a somersault. But where Muybridge’s grid isolates each phase of the action with scientific detachment, Moreno Sánchez’s figures overlap and collide. The bodies are not those of trained acrobats. Their legs are bent, their balance is precarious. The figure on the left seems to have collapsed entirely, a heap of limbs dissolving back into the grass. The pleasure here is not in the perfection of the form but in the messy, exuberant act of attempting it, of giving in to a moment of gravitational chaos.

The primary risk of such a painting is slightness. The watercolour is an observation made on a single sheet of paper, with much of the ground left bare. The figures are defined by simple, almost naive outlines, their faces either turned away or lost in shadow. The work could be dismissed as a sketch, a preparatory thought. Yet its very lack of finish is what gives it its force. It proposes that this is enough. This watercolour, painted more than a decade before the dense, monstrous, and digitally-sourced bodies of a canvas like "Catástrofe en el bosque", is a statement of faith in the provisional. It captures a moment of physical unselfconsciousness that feels entirely alien to the world of his later paintings.

The painting addresses a freedom that is incidental and bodily rather than grand or heroic. This freedom belongs to a Sunday afternoon when no one is watching, when the only purpose is the feeling of grass under your hands and the brief, dizzying spectacle of the world flipping over. Seurat’s figures are on display, performing their leisure for each other and for us. Moreno Sánchez’s figures are performing for no one. Their faces are hidden by the very motion that defines them. We are not shown their identities, only the simple, unburdened fact of their play.
