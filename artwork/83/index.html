Fin de curso.

2015, Watercolor on paper, 15 x 20 cm.

The Two Languages

A painting can speak two languages at once. One language can describe the weight and warmth of a body, the weather of a private feeling, rendered in marks that are themselves fluid and contingent. A second language can describe the world that body lives in, but as a system, a pattern, a thing of rules and repetitions. Most pictures choose one tongue and hold to it. The tension in the work of Juanma Moreno Sánchez, even in a small watercolor like this one from 2015, often comes from what happens when a painting insists on speaking both at the same time.

Here, the two languages are assigned their territories with absolute clarity. The human figures are all heat and light. They are rendered in hot, unstable washes of orange, yellow, and red, the watercolor allowed to pool and bleed so that their bodies feel less like bounded shapes and more like centers of thermal energy. Their intimacy, the way one head rests against another, is a physical fact described by the wet-on-wet blurring of their forms. The blurring of their forms speaks the language of skin, of touch, of a connection so close that the boundaries between two people begin to dissolve. The painting reveals a world of feeling, given its proper, fluid grammar.

The world around them speaks a different dialect entirely. The background is a cool, flat, systematic field. A pale grey-blue wash provides the ground for a dense, rising cloud of dark green dots. The dots are not an Impressionist’s attempt to capture light. They are a pattern, a screen, a handmade piece of code. The field of dots is not a real place, not a lawn or a starlit night. The pattern is a non-space, a purely decorative field that functions much like the flat, glittering gold backgrounds of the Byzantine mosaics at Ravenna. That gold was the colour of heaven, a divine ether that lifted the figures of emperors and saints out of the mundane world. Moreno Sánchez’s field of dots does something similar: it isolates the human event, seals it off from any specific location, and presents it as a self-contained icon of intimacy. The title, Fin de curso (End of Term), suggests a moment of quiet connection poised before a coming dispersal, a private farewell before the world rushes back in.

This formal division, between the organic human and the systematic world, is a problem Moreno Sánchez will return to again and again. What is here a handmade pattern of dots will, years later, evolve into the shredded digital noise of a painting like Jardín triturado or the generative strangeness of the bodies themselves in works like Ángel III. This small watercolor from 2015 is an early, quiet statement of the theme. The watercolor is a painting about the friction between the warmth of the body and the coldness of the systems that contain it, a theme that feels deeply contemporary but is here rendered with a tenderness that feels almost timeless, like a much later, more secular answer to the quiet shared feeling of his 2016 Abrazo.

The risk in such a clean division of labour, between the figures that feel and the background that thinks, is that the painting becomes a diagram of an idea. The separation is so complete that the two languages might fail to create a single, unified poem. The work’s emotional charge is undeniable, but it is so perfectly insulated by the patterned field around it, so hermetically sealed, that one is left to wonder if the connection is too perfect, the solution too neat. I am not entirely sure the painting survives the risk.

Then you look again. The dots are not just a background. They seem to emanate from the figures themselves, rising from their shoulders like a cloud of static, a swarm of unspoken thoughts, or the atoms of the world being reassembled around them. The system, it turns out, might not be separate from the bodies at all. It may be coming from them. In that ambiguity, the painting’s neat diagram comes beautifully undone.
