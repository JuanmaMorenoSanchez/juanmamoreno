Niño-dios aprieta pero no ahoga.

2012, Oil on canvas, 146 x 146 cm.

The God Who Might Drop You

To paint a god now is to make a choice about belief. Not the painter’s own, which is his business, but the belief of the world he is painting for. An artist who reaches for the old machinery of the sacred, for the halo and the holy light, cannot assume a shared faith will complete the circuit. He has to build the doubt into the picture itself. The question of whether this power is real, whether it is benevolent, whether it is paying any attention at all, becomes the engine of the work. The painting must stage not a faith, but an argument with faith, and leave the verdict open.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Niño-dios aprieta pero no ahoga from 2012 is a masterful, unsettling answer to this problem. The title is a direct revision of a Spanish proverb, “Dios aprieta pero no ahoga” (God squeezes, but does not strangle), a folk reassurance that divine power, however painful, is survivable. By substituting Niño-dios (Child-god), Moreno Sánchez corrupts the guarantee. A child’s grip is clumsy. A child’s attention wanders. A child might squeeze and not realize his own strength. The painting gives us this terrifying deity in the form of a boy whose face is beatific and whose curls are backlit into a perfect halo. He sits in the landscape of an old master painting at dusk, making a gesture of benediction, but he is dressed in a cheap, contemporary striped polo shirt. The collision of the sacred apparatus with the utterly mundane uniform is the first crack in the painting’s surface.

The second is the bird. In the tradition of Spanish sacred painting this canvas argues with, an infant Christ might hold a goldfinch, a symbol of his future Passion. This child-god holds a common pigeon, and he holds it just a little too firmly. The bird is the entire theological drama of the painting made flesh. Its pose is not one of gentle rest but of tense submission. This work's nearest ancestor is found in the humble, anonymous tradition of the Spanish ex-voto, rather than in the grand altarpieces of the Baroque. An ex-voto is a painting made as a thank-you note, a small, often crudely painted scene depicting the moment a saint intervened to save someone from disaster. This painting is the inverse. The painting is not the record of a prayer answered. The canvas is a portrait of the power being prayed to, revealing that power to be distracted, smug, and potentially lethal in its carelessness.

This canvas from 2012 stands as a crucial pivot in Moreno Sánchez’s work. A decade before he would begin generating monstrous and holy bodies with algorithms, he was already stress-testing the vocabulary of the sacred. The concern with bodies caught in inexplicable situations, seen in other paintings from the same year like El silencio (I) or the brutal scene in Carne, is here given a theological frame. So too is the unsettling mix of religious iconography and contemporary life that he would later push to its limit in works like the profane and glorious Asunción of 2015. Here, the elements are held in a more precarious balance.

The painting’s greatest risk is the boy’s face itself. The face is rendered with a smoothness, an almost sentimental sweetness, that feels at odds with the looser, more agitated brushwork of the ground and the landscape. The expression is so placid it verges on the generic, the face of a child from a stock photograph rather than a portrait sitting. For a painter so adept at capturing character, the choice feels deliberate. Perhaps this is the painting’s most critical insight: that a truly arbitrary power would not have a face charged with personality or malice. It would have the blank, faintly smiling face of someone who is not really thinking about you at all. The sweetness of the face is what makes the cruelty of the grip so chilling.

The enduring impression is the pressure of the boy’s fingers on the bird. Is it a hold of affection or control? Is the animal a pet or a victim? The painting never answers, because the child-god himself may not have decided. He looks out, past us, his gesture holding our attention while his mind is elsewhere. The entire promise of salvation or threat of annihilation rests in the nerves of a small hand, and the painting leaves us suspended in the long, quiet moment before we find out if it will squeeze, or let go.
