La estudiante Erasmus.

2011, Oil on canvas, 100 x 81 cm.

The View from Nowhere

To paint a figure looking out over a landscape is to walk into a trap set by the Romantics two hundred years ago. The inheritance is Caspar David Friedrich’s Wanderer above the Sea of Fog, a painting that has become the definitive image of man mastering a sublime view. The painter who attempts this subject now must reckon with that man on the precipice, his back to us, his gaze conquering the world. The work starts with a choice: to repeat that heroic story, or to find a way to tell a different one. Juanma Moreno Sánchez, in this canvas from 2011, finds a different one. His painting is an argument with the entire premise of the sublime.

The title, La estudiante Erasmus, tells you who this figure is. She is not a wanderer taking possession of the wilderness; she is a student, temporarily displaced, perched on a ledge overlooking a city she does not own. She is hunched, her body folded into a crouch, her gaze directed down and inward, not out toward the horizon. The grand classical column framing the right side of the canvas seems to belong to another story, a fragment of an old order she sits beside but does not participate in. The work is not a portrait of someone mastering the world with their vision. The painting instead portrays a person’s quiet, self-contained presence within it.

Moreno Sánchez’s work from this period often circles the solitary figure and the textures of a life in progress. The anomie here feels like a public extension of the cluttered interiors he painted in works like Piso de estudiantes, from 2007. The same sense of a world being navigated, not conquered, is there. It has an ambition that feels larger and more sustained than the fleeting, snapshot quality of his earlier street scenes like Transeúntes, but it shares their interest in anonymous passage. Compared to another 2011 painting, the quiet interior of Trenza de Patri, this canvas swaps a shared, private silence for a profound public loneliness. The figure is outside, surrounded by a million other lives, and utterly alone.

The painting’s great risk is its own drama. The sky is a turbulent, theatrical affair of deep teals and bruised clouds, punctuated by what might be a shooting star. It threatens to tip the mood into cinematic melancholy, a backdrop for a feeling rather than the source of one. A lesser painting would be consumed by it. Here, though, the theatricality of the sky is undercut by the sheer mundanity of the figure. Her posture is temporary and informal. Her bright white trainers, a detail of unapologetic contemporaneity, anchor her to the ledge. Her quietness feels more real than the sky’s noisy performance, and in that tension the painting finds its center. It may be that the grandeur of the view is just a thing that is happening, and her own thoughts are the real event.

This kind of urban solitude finds a different echo in the work of the American Ashcan School painters, who a century earlier turned their attention to the ordinary life of the city. Artists like John Sloan painted women on tenement rooftops, finding freedom and unselfconscious life in those high-up spaces. They were oases from the cramped life below. For Moreno Sánchez’s student, the height offers not liberation but a form of contemplative detachment. She is not part of a community sharing the open air. She is isolated, a watcher. I cannot know what occupies her thoughts, and the painting is stronger for refusing to speculate. Its subject is not her story, but the state of being alone with a view.

The whole city is rendered in a deep, ambiguous darkness, a suggestion of architecture rather than a map. But in that darkness, just below the student’s outstretched hand, one window is brightly lit. The window is a small square of impossible warmth, a tiny signal of a life happening in one of those rooms. The light is a declaration of another world, another story, that she can see but not enter. That light is a single point of human specificity in the anonymous mass of the city, and it is everything she is not. The painting leaves her there, perched between the indifferent architecture and the vast sky, a temporary figure contemplating a world that will go on without her.
