Limbo.

2016, Watercolor on paper, 32 x 45 cm.

The Gravity of a Situation

To paint a leap is to risk a cliché. The gesture comes so loaded with a history of cheap joy, of stock-photograph freedom, that to render it with a straight face requires a steady nerve. A painter can frame it as a joke, or as a tragedy, but to paint a leap as simply a leap is to invite ridicule. The subject is a trap. What, then, if the leap takes them over a void rather than into open air? What if the ground below is not solid but has been replaced by a circle of absolute darkness? The problem changes. The subject is no longer freedom, but something closer to faith, or perhaps, desperation.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s watercolor from 2016 gives this problem a title: Limbo. The name itself argues against a simple reading of release. The title suggests a condition of being caught between states rather than a destination. Two figures, their bodies a mosaic of warm pinks and cool blues, are held mid-air. One body clings to the other, a knot of trust against a sky of corrosive, chemical yellow. The ground is a wash of sour green. The color scheme evokes a purely pictorial space, a world reduced to an emblem, rather than the naturalism of a summer's day. The watercolor, with its inherent fluidity and refusal of hard edges, makes the entire scene feel provisional, as if it might dissolve at any moment. The one point of absolute finality is the thing they are jumping over: a round, dark abyss that bleeds into the green, pulling the eye down.

This painting marks a quiet pivot in his work. It shares its year with more legible scenes of contemporary life, like the found moment of a snapshot in Say cheese, but it rejects their social grounding. Earlier watercolors, like the furtive public dramas of Voyeur or Carnaval en la periferia, find their tension in the awkwardness of shared space. Limbo sheds the details of the world to stage a more elemental conflict. It strips away the scenery, the specific time and place, to create a private mythology. The painting is an act of distillation. The countless anxieties of the present are boiled down to a single, primal image: a bond, a leap, a void.

The image of a soul’s perilous journey over an abyss has a long, dark history in painting, one concerned not with joy, but with judgment. It feels less like a modern leap and more like a medieval trial. In Simon Marmion's celebrated illuminations for The Visions of the Knight Tondal from 1475, the errant knight’s soul is guided through a series of hellish landscapes. One trial involves crossing a terrifying void on a narrow, precarious bridge, a test of faith in a world of tangible horrors. Moreno Sánchez’s watercolor feels like a secular descendant of that vision. He removes the elaborate monsters, the guiding angel, and the Christian promise of salvation or damnation. The theological scaffolding is gone. All that remains is the human test: two figures, bound together, attempting to clear a darkness that has no name and offers no explanation.

There is a risk, of course, that the symbolism lands too neatly, that the painting becomes a diagram of a feeling instead of its embodiment. We assume the figures are a mother and child, and the bond a protective one, but the painting withholds their faces, their intentions left entirely opaque. Is the embrace a rescue, or is one pulling the other down? The entire work balances on this knife-edge of ambiguity. Its power depends entirely on whether that dark circle at the bottom registers as a genuine mystery or just a hole in the paper. The strange alchemy of watercolor allows this darker wash, this simple absence of light, to exert such a powerful downward pull.

The painting sets up an argument between the upward energy of the leap and the undeniable gravity of the void. The thinness of the watercolor, the way the light of the paper shines through the toxic colors, lends a fragility to the whole affair. Nothing here feels permanent. The figures are not frozen in a heroic pose; they are caught in a moment of profound instability. They are left hanging, which is what the title promised from the very beginning.
