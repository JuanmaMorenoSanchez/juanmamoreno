Verano sombrío II.

2013, Watercolor on paper, 22 x 16 cm.

The Fault Line

A painting can declare its allegiance to the figure, or it can surrender to pure colour. It can build a window onto a recognisable world, or it can insist on its own flat surface, on its status as a coloured object. The particular unease of this small watercolour comes from its attempt to do both at once, and from its refusal to reconcile them. The work is built on a geological fault line. Two different modes of painting have been forced into the same frame, and the tension of their meeting holds the image in a state of quiet, structural crisis.

The top two-thirds of the paper are an exercise in landscape as pure mood. Thick, horizontal bands of dark indigo, brilliant ochre, and deep ocean blue are stacked like slabs of atmosphere. The somberness of the title, Verano sombrío II, comes not from any one colour, since the yellow burns with an intense heat, but from the deliberate disjunction between these planes. This is not a sky above a sea; these are two separate realities laid one on top of the other. Pinned to the very bottom, almost falling out of the composition, is a third reality: a small figure rendered in the traditional language of a beach scene. Her back is to us, her purpose unknown. She is a fragment of a story that has drifted into the wrong frame.

This schism marks a crucial point in Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s work. A year earlier, in 2012, he had produced a series of tiny, joyous watercolors called Verano luminoso. They are studies in pure light, confident and almost entirely abstract. With Verano sombrío II, the figure returns, posing a problem that had been absent from those earlier works. Can the human form, which had grounded his paintings for years in pieces like the early "Piso de estudiantes", survive in this new world of hard-edged abstraction? Three years later, in Verano sombrío I, he would return to the theme, offering a different and more corrosive answer. This 2013 painting, however, holds the question open, staging the conflict as a structural impasse.

The American painter Milton Avery spent a career proving this conflict could be resolved. In beach scenes like Swimmers and Sunbathers (1945), figures and landscapes dissolve into one another, simplified into flat, harmonious planes of colour. Avery created a unified world where the human form and the abstract field could coexist peacefully. Moreno Sánchez’s watercolor, made nearly seventy years later, functions as a direct and pessimistic response to that synthesis. He does not smooth over the seam between his two modes of painting; he makes the seam the subject. The clean, hard edge where the yellow sky meets the blue water is the most important line in the painting, the place where one kind of art stops and another begins.

The risk, of course, is that the work becomes a diagram of its own argument, a thesis statement about two kinds of seeing instead of an immersive world. The division is perhaps too cleanly stated, the problem presented with a clarity that forecloses mystery. Whether the figure is a child at play or an adult in contemplation is a detail the painting withholds, and her refusal to show her face is a refusal to anchor the scene with a specific emotion. She simply sits there, a piece of evidence. Her blonde hair, a small explosion of light against the murky brown of the ground, has more in common with the brilliant ochre of the sky than with the earth she occupies, a sky she cannot see and to which she cannot belong. She seems less a part of a landscape and more a specimen trapped at the bottom of it.
