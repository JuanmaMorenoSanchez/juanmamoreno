Urinario.

2015, Watercolor on paper, 20 x 15 cm.

The Confession Box

A door in a painting is a promise of another space. For centuries, painters have used it to suggest a world beyond the one we see, a threshold between the known and the unknown, the mundane and the marvellous. Sometimes, however, a door is just a door. It opens not onto another world, but onto a small, functional, and deeply un-magical box. The subject of painting then becomes the collision between the grand promise of the frame and the prosaic reality of what it contains. A painting can find its purpose in that disappointment.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Urinario is just such a painting. The title is a direct, almost brutal, statement of fact, naming the most functional and unpoetic of objects. It immediately invokes the ghost of Duchamp and his Fountain, a gesture that sought to drain the art object of its aura. Yet Moreno Sánchez’s small watercolor, made over a century later, does precisely the opposite. The crude plastic of the portable toilet is rendered in a deep, devotional red, and its edges are traced with a glittering, nervous line of gold. It floats in an absolute, featureless darkness, lit from within like a lantern. The treatment is that of a sacred object, a reliquary, or the candle-lit niche of a minor saint in a seventeenth-century Dutch nocturne. The work of a painter like Godfried Schalcken, with his mastery of small scenes carved out of darkness by a single flame, finds a strange echo here. This is not the profane object made art; this is the profane object painted as if it were already holy.

The watercolor belongs to a specific and powerful inquiry in his work from 2015. That year, he produced a series of paintings that tested the capacity of religious language. A messy, ambiguous watercolor of two figures became a Piedad, and a figure in a denim jacket and flip-flops rising from the sea was titled Asunción. Urinario is the quietest and perhaps most potent of the set. It proposes that the vocabulary of the sacred (of the glowing aura, the private chamber, the single illuminated figure) can be applied to the most temporary and disposable architecture of modern life. It finds a private chapel in the plastic box at a music festival, a place of brief, necessary solitude within the anonymous crowd suggested by other works like Festival.

The man is caught at the threshold, neither fully in nor out. We cannot quite tell what he is doing, or what he holds in his hand. It might be a phone, its screen providing a second, smaller source of light, but the painting withholds that certainty. He is simply held in a moment of private transaction, his face turned away from us and toward the interior of his temporary shelter. The box becomes a confession booth for a secular age, a space for a ritual that has no name, its plastic walls momentarily granted the significance of ancient stone. The darkness surrounding it is not empty space; it is the world from which this figure has briefly retreated.

Still, the painting takes a risk that it does not entirely survive. The gold and silver line-work that gives the object its halo feels, in places, like a graphic shortcut. It is an applied sanctity, a decorative flourish that sits on top of the watercolor rather than emerging from it. This decorative impulse slightly undermines the painting’s transformative work, threatening to turn the object into a stylized illustration of itself rather than a thing remade by the act of looking. Yet this flaw is also what makes the work honest. It shows the effort. The painting does not claim the transformation is complete or easy. It gives us an object of profound banality and attempts to bless it, and the strain of that attempt is where the work truly lives: in the space between the grubby plastic and the golden light.
