Mareo en el hidropedal.

2013, Watercolor on paper, 22 x 15 cm.

The Unwell Light

To paint the summer is to enter an argument with light. For a Spanish painter, it is an argument with the ghost of Joaquín Sorolla, with the memory of a Mediterranean rendered as an explosion of brilliance, a world where light dissolves form and radiates pure joy. Sorolla painted monumental canvases outdoors, chasing the transient effects of a sun that bleaches colour from the sand and glitters on the skin of children. His project was one of total, dazzling immersion. The problem for a painter now is that this total immersion feels like a story from another time. The light is still there, but the uncomplicated joy it once promised has grown suspect. An honest painting of the summer might now have to account for a certain sickness that gets into the glare.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s small watercolor from 2013, Mareo en el hidropedal (Dizziness on the pedal boat), is just such an account. It presents the props of a perfect holiday: a figure in swimwear, a drink sweating in her hand, a pedal boat with a slide waiting on the water. But the painting refuses the pleasure. The title gives us the diagnosis, an internal feeling of nausea. The image provides the visual evidence. This world is unwell. Instead of Valencia's sun-bleached blue, the sky is a heavy, bruised band of crimson pressing down. Instead of a shimmering surface of reflected light, the sea is an opaque, inert block of blue. These are not descriptions of a place. They are the architecture of a bad feeling, color as a physical symptom.

The painting’s method, its division of the scene into simple, flat planes of color, finds its ancestors not in the Spanish tradition of light, but elsewhere. It belongs to a line of modernism that runs through a painter like Milton Avery, an American who also simplified the world into its essential color shapes. Avery’s beach scenes, like Swimmers and Sunbathers (1945), reduce figures and landscapes to interlocking fields of color, creating a mood that is often quiet, strange, and subtly melancholic. Moreno Sánchez takes this language of radical simplification and pushes it towards a more explicit state of disquiet. Where Avery’s color creates a serene, abstract harmony, the colors here are in a state of silent conflict. The heavy red and the flat blue do not resolve; they sit together uneasily, framing a leisure that has curdled into dread.

Made in 2013, this watercolor belongs to a moment of transition in Moreno Sánchez's work. It marks a clear souring of the idylls he had painted only a year before. His "Verano luminoso" watercolors from 2012, like Verano luminoso I, are quick, light-filled sketches of summer ease. By the time he paints Mareo en el hidropedal and its companion piece Verano sombrío II, the mood has decisively turned. The light has developed a fever. This work also stands as a precursor to the digital exhaustion of a later painting like 0% battery, but here the malaise feels less technological and more atmospheric, a sickness in the air itself. It is a portrait of a very specific, modern feeling: the anxiety that can bloom in the heart of mandated fun.

The painting’s main risk is that it becomes too much of an illustration. The pedal boat, floating in the middle distance, is rendered with a simplicity that verges on the schematic. The pedal boat is an almost too-perfect symbol of cheap, organized leisure. I am not entirely convinced that its slight awkwardness, its toylike quality, fully escapes the danger of being a simple caption for the work’s thesis. Does this naivety ground the painting's sophisticated color-play in a recognizable reality, or does it flatten the work's mystery into a more legible diagram of unease? The question remains open. The work’s strength lies in its willingness to take this risk, to operate so close to the edge of allegory.

The painting’s entire contradiction is held in the glass the woman is holding. The glass is a small world of vivid red, dotted with the dark shapes of berries, a concentration of potential sweetness and pleasure. She holds this small, contained promise against a world that offers none. Her body is turned away from us, her gaze fixed on the sick landscape. She is caught between the drink in her hand and the dizziness in her head, and the painting leaves her there, suspended in the unbearable brightness of a summer day that has gone wrong.
