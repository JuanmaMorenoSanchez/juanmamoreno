Comedor de gambas.

2009, Oil on canvas, 61 x 50 cm.

The Circle of Operations

A painter can show a person alone in a crowd, and we understand it as loneliness. A painter can show a person engaged with others, and we read it as connection. What Juanma Moreno Sánchez finds in this canvas from 2009 is a third state, one harder to name: a willed invisibility. It is not the sadness of being overlooked, but the deliberate act of making oneself absent from the room by sheer force of concentration. The work of looking begins with the world he has chosen to shut out, rather than with the figure.

That world is a hot, crimson smear, a social event rendered as pure noise. The figures in the background have no faces, only a kind of urgent, blurred velocity. They are the party the man has refused. In response, he has created a circle of operations for himself, a zone of cool, brightly-lit clarity on the white tablecloth. Here, in this private arena, he is absorbed in a single, delicate task. Moreno Sánchez’s early work is often a study in social unease, a close reading of the awkward spaces between people, as in the shared but separate paths of his 2008 painting Transeúntes. In Comedor de gambas, the solution to that unease is to erect a wall of intense focus, turning the simple act of eating into a ritual that leaves no room for the outside world to enter. His crisp white shirt is the uniform for a social occasion, but it has been stained by the work, a single red mark of visceral contact.

There is a long, transversal line in painting that finds a kind of secular holiness in the act of intense concentration. It runs from the Dutch Golden Age painters of scholars in their studies down to this Spanish restaurant. The subject finds a surprising ancestor in the work of a fijnschilder (a "fine painter") like Gerrit Dou, whose Astronomer by Candlelight from the 1660s shows a man hunched over a celestial globe, his face illuminated by his focus while the rest of the world falls into darkness. Moreno Sánchez borrows this grammar of absorption, but applies it to a subject that is radically prosaic. The cosmos has shrunk to a plate of shrimp. The astronomer’s globe becomes a prawn’s curled body. The act of devotion remains, a dedicated and almost scientific inquiry into a tiny, temporary piece of the world.

Does the painting’s mundane subject buckle under the weight of such serious treatment? Perhaps. The title, Comedor de gambas (Shrimp Eater), is bluntly descriptive, refusing any poetic lift. The painting’s tension lies right there, in the gap between the grand, art-historical machinery of its composition and the slightness of the event it records. This tension is a risk the canvas takes openly. This willingness to find gravity in the vernacular, in the disposable moment, is a constant in his work from this period, visible in his treatment of a low-resolution digital image in Stolen pic II from the same year. Moreno Sánchez seems to be asking what happens when the most intense and meaningful human experiences are no longer found in grand events, but must be located in small, private, and sometimes messy rituals.

The focus of the operation is in the hands. They are delicate, precise, and utterly absorbed in their task. This is a body under complete control, a tool wielded by a focused mind to dismantle another, smaller body. This controlled, purposeful flesh is a world away from the chaotic, generative anatomies that would appear in his work more than a decade later, as in the tangled, surplus limbs of Ángel III. In this 2009 canvas, the body is not the site of the uncanny; it is the instrument used to keep the world at bay. The painting is a portrait of a mind using a physical act to maintain its own perfect, defended solitude.

The last thing you might notice is the other cutlery on the table, still neatly wrapped in its napkin, unused. He is eating with his fingers. Within the civilized construction of the restaurant, on the clean white cloth of his operational theatre, he has opted for the most direct, primal contact. The entire drama of the painting is in that choice: an animal act of consumption, performed with the meticulous concentration of a watchmaker.
