Juego absurdo.

2020, Oil on canvas, 130 x 163 cm.

The Dignity of the Game

To paint a group of people is to make a claim about how we live together. Over the years, Juanma Moreno Sánchez has often painted the group as a fractured or failed unit. It appears as a scene of profound disconnection in the snowbound arguments of El silencio (I), or as a cabal of faceless executives enacting a mythic boardroom malice in Hijos de puta. Even his crowds are collections of private worlds that fail to connect. This painting, from 2020, proposes a different possibility. It offers a group that works. A gathering of young people, whose faces are mostly obscured or turned away, collaborates on a single, pointless, and entirely absorbing task: holding up a giant sheet of red fabric.

The fabric is the painting’s argument. It becomes a piece of temporary architecture, a self-made crimson roof against a pale sky. For a moment, an exterior scene becomes an interior, a shared shelter built not of bricks but of focused, collective effort. This structure is fluid and alive, the opposite of the rigid, symbolic architecture that separates worlds in a painting like his earlier El templo. Here, the sacred space is the one the figures make for themselves, a canopy whose only function is to exist for a moment and then collapse. The game is absurd, as the title suggests, but the concentration is serious. Every arm is raised, every hand has found its grip, holding the billowing form just clear of their heads.

This gesture has a long and serious history. A canopy held aloft over a procession, a baldachin, has for centuries been a sign of sanctity, a portable sky for a holy object or person. A work like The Dawn Procession (ca. 1860) by the 19th-century Spanish painter Eugenio Lucas Velázquez shows the convention: a frantic, crowded scene where the canopy attempts to protect a religious icon from the chaos of the masses. Moreno Sánchez takes this formal device of veneration and gives it to children as a toy. The crimson fabric is a secular baldachin, and the object it sanctifies is nothing more than the space the group occupies together. The sacredness is found in the act of collaboration itself. It is a quiet, radical repurposing of a visual language, taking a symbol of power and giving it back as a form of play.

The painting’s date, 2020, is crucial. This is the same year Moreno Sánchez was deep into his unsettling explorations of figures born from generative algorithms, producing the beautifully monstrous bodies of the Monstruo del espacio latente series and the unreadable, biomechanical forms of works like Madonna (I). While one part of his studio was wrestling with the post-human, this canvas was affirming a purely human connection. It stands as a control group, a baseline for what a painting of people can do without recourse to the digitally strange. Placed against the uncanny bodies he was making elsewhere, this simple, physical game feels like an anchor, a statement of faith in the integrity of a shared physical world.

Yet a risk remains. The painting is so direct in its feeling that it verges on a sweetness the rest of his work carefully avoids. Perhaps the small patch of warm, orange cloud in the upper right is a concession, a touch of conventional sunset beauty in a canvas that is otherwise so grounded in the awkward mechanics of the game. I cannot be sure whether this detail is a deliberate note of grace or a crack in the painting's otherwise tough-minded surface. The ambiguity is worth holding onto.

The final force of the work resides in its surfaces, and in what they refuse to show. We see the worn denim, the tracksuits, the bare feet on the strange, red-shadowed ground. We do not see clear faces or legible expressions. The figures are defined by their posture and their physical effort. The subject is the shared, muscular business of holding this temporary world together. The painting finds a profound dignity in that shared, useless, and absolutely necessary work.
