Autorretrato con Puri (II).

2019, Oil on board, 40 x 30 cm.

A Small Blue World

A portrait is an act of presentation, a face turned toward the world. A self-portrait, doubly so. But so much of modern life is an act of turning away, of narrowing our focus to a small, glowing rectangle, that a portrait of pure outwardness can feel like a lie. Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s paintings often document the strange negotiations of the self in public, as in the cheerful, weary performance of Otro puto selfie (2014). This small, intense painting from 2019 proposes a different kind of portrait. The self is here, but it is bent inward, its gaze directed not at us, nor at the landscape, but down into the solid blue mystery of a cup.

The gesture is one of profound concentration. The artist’s back is stooped, his hands forming a stable cradle for the object of his attention. The posture recalls the field labourer or the monk at prayer. He is looking for something. The cup itself is the most definite thing in the painting, a block of pure, flat, assertive colour against the gestural energy of the world around it. The blue is denser and more absolute than the pale wash of the sky it sits beneath. It feels less like a vessel for holding a drink and more like a tool for looking, a scrying glass for a secular age. The focus is so complete that the body performing it almost seems to dematerialise; the paint that describes the artist's shirt and hair is loose and hurried, as if the painter’s real interest was in capturing the quality of the attention, not the architecture of the body providing it.

And then there is the cat. A quiet, living weight draped across the artist’s shoulders, Puri is the painting’s anchor to the real. The animal is a passenger, its gaze turned vaguely aside, utterly indifferent to the small ritual taking place below.

This act of profound withdrawal into a private mental space has a long history in art. When Antonello da Messina painted St. Jerome in His Study around 1475, he depicted the saint as a figure of immense learning, sealed inside a beautifully constructed bubble of concentration. Jerome has his books, his desk, and his lion (which, the story goes, followed him around like a house cat) all neatly arranged, a small world of thought carved out of the larger architecture of a church. Moreno Sánchez gives us a contemporary, outdoor version of that same impulse. The study is now a restless field, rendered in strokes of deep green over a very specific, insistent orange that seems to burn from beneath the surface. The scholar’s text is an empty cup. The lion is a tabby cat. The painting is one of four from 2019 that worry at this same problem, this intimate pact between man and animal, exploring it from different angles in Autorretrato con Puri (I), III, and IV. The theme of an unlikely, powerful bond with an animal witness goes back further, to the strange tenderness of his 2010 watercolor, A friend.

The painting risks a certain instability. The figure of the artist, caught between the solid fact of the cat and the vibrating energy of the landscape, feels less resolved than either. The brushwork that builds his form is quick, almost impatient. This is a critical choice, not a failure. The work is not, finally, a portrait of a person. It is a portrait of a gesture. The painting is less interested in who the man is than in the act of looking he performs with such quiet, total commitment. It is a work about the search, not the searcher.

We are left watching him watch. The landscape rushes and churns around him, the cat rides along, and the man keeps his eyes fixed on the small blue world in his hands. What he finds there, or hopes to find, is a secret the painting keeps. The work’s quiet power lies in this withholding. It doesn’t offer a revelation, only a faithful record of the moment just before one, a testament to the deeply human need to look for meaning in the most ordinary of containers.
