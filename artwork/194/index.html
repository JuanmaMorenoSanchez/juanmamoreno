Piso de estudiantes.

2007, Oil on canvas, 162 x 116 cm.

On the ceilings of Baroque churches, painters like Andrea Pozzo dissolved solid architecture into an illusion of endless sky, casting saints and angels into a paradise of pure perspectival mathematics. It was a demonstration of divine power that made light of earthly physics. Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s early canvas Piso de estudiantes starts from something similar, a body floating untethered in space, but what it sets up is completely different. The figure’s movement is an ascent toward the heavens interrupted by a ceiling.

A young man floats in a vortex of warped architectural planes, arms raised in a gesture halfway between surrender and performance. He is calm. His face is turned upward, as if looking toward the light source of this impossible room. Whether he is falling, jumping, or being thrown is something the painting keeps to itself. The emotional key lies below, in the tangle of bodies that forms the bottom third of the canvas. There is no alarm here, only an easy, smiling warmth. One of the figures, eyes closed in what looks like pleasure, receives a gentle hand on their head. The scene does not depict a disaster, but a game. The title roots this grand, collapsing geometry in the mundane reality of a shared flat, a place where the rules of the world are temporarily suspended for games born out of boredom.

Painted in 2007, this canvas marks an important early point in Moreno Sánchez’s work. It establishes an early and lasting interest in the ambiguous dynamics of groups, a subject he would return to in looser, more anecdotal watercolor studies also titled Piso de estudiantes from 2009, and in larger canvases about collective leisure such as Veinteañeros en la Feria de San Mateo (2010). In those paintings, as here, the story is never entirely clear. The energy belongs to those moments that feel important, yet remain unreadable to anyone watching from outside. The drama in Piso de estudiantes is not narrative, but physical: a matter of weight, balance, and the implicit trust that someone will catch you.

Where Pozzo’s famous ceiling in the Church of Saint Ignatius in Rome uses a fictitious vanishing point to create a sense of boundless order, Moreno Sánchez’s composition collapses inward. The light is a domestic yellow, not a celestial white, and the space feels less like a miracle than a cramped room made vast by the energy inside it. It borrows the formal language of impossible perspective, but applies it to a secular, deeply personal subject. The painting makes a grand, Baroque statement out of a small, human feeling: the fleeting, reckless faith that your friends will not let you hit the floor.

The floating figure's pose is almost too graceful to be an accident, closer to a dancer's controlled descent than a chaotic fall. The connection between his airborne body and the tender clutter below is conceptual rather than painterly; hands are there, delicate, but none are positioned to cushion a landing. Perhaps that is the point. All the dizzying angles and sinking shadows, all the painted architecture supporting the scene, are anchored by the most concrete detail in the frame: the closed-eyed smile of the figure below. That smile is the emotional ground in a scene that has done away with gravity. It confirms that the game is safe, and that falling is an act of joy.
