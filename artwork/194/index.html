Piso de estudiantes.

2007, Oil on canvas on cardboard, 162 x 116 cm.

The Physics of Trust

On the ceilings of baroque churches, painters like Andrea Pozzo dissolved solid architecture into an illusion of infinite sky, launching saints and angels into a paradise of pure perspectival mathematics. It was a demonstration of divine power that mocked earthly physics. An early canvas by Juanma Moreno Sánchez, Piso de estudiantes, starts from a similar transgression, a body floating untethered in space, but what it suggests is completely different. The figure’s movement is an ascent to the heavens interrupted by a ceiling.

A young man floats in a vortex of twisted architectural planes, his arms raised in a gesture halfway between surrender and performance. He is serene. His face is turned upward, as if looking at the source of light in this impossible room. Whether he is falling, jumping or being thrown is a detail the painting keeps to itself. The emotional key is below, in the jumble of bodies that makes up the bottom third of the canvas. There is no alarm here, only a serene, smiling warmth. One of the figures, eyes closed in what seems to be a gesture of pleasure, receives a soft hand on their head. The scene does not depict a catastrophe, but a game. The title places this grand, collapsing geometry in the prosaic reality of a shared flat, a place where the rules of the world are temporarily suspended for games born from boredom.

Painted in 2007, this canvas is an important milestone at the beginning of Moreno Sánchez’s work. It establishes an early and lasting interest in the ambiguous dynamics of groups of people, a subject he would return to in the looser, more anecdotal watercolour studies, also titled Piso de estudiantes from 2009, and in larger canvases about collective leisure like Veinteañeros en la Feria de San Mateo (2010). In these paintings, as here, the story is never entirely clear. The energy is that of moments that seem important but whose meaning is illegible to an outsider. The drama in Piso de estudiantes is not narrative but physical: a matter of weight, balance, and the implicit faith that you will be caught.

Where Pozzo’s famous ceiling in the Church of St. Ignatius in Rome uses a fictional vanishing point to create an impression of limitless order, Moreno Sánchez’s composition collapses inward. The light is a domestic yellow, not a celestial white, and the space seems less a miracle than a narrow room made immense by the energy within it. It borrows the formal language of impossible perspective but applies it to a secular and deeply intimate subject. The painting makes a grand, baroque statement about a small, human feeling: the momentary, reckless faith that your friends will not let you hit the floor.

The floating figure’s pose is almost too elegant to be a mere accident, closer to the controlled fall of a dancer than to a chaotic collapse. The connection between his airborne body and the tender jumble below is conceptual, not a pictorial fact; the hands are delicate and they are there, but none is in a position to break a fall. Perhaps that is the point. All the dizzying angles and sinking shadows, all the pictorial architecture that holds the scene up, are anchored by the most concrete thing in the frame: the closed-eyed smile of the figure at the bottom. The smile is the emotional ground in a scene that has abolished gravity. It confirms that the game is safe, and that the fall is an act of joy.
