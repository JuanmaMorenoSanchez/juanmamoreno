Realidad virtual.

2025, Watercolor on paper, 29,7 x 21 cm.

The Competing Ghosts

A landscape painting used to make a simple promise: that it could take you somewhere. Through the window of the frame, it offered a world to be entered, a unified place whose light and weather and earth you could almost feel. The bargain was one of imaginative transport. A recurring problem in Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s work is what happens when the figures inside the painting refuse that bargain, when they stand in a world but decline to arrive. They have made other arrangements.

His 2025 watercolor, Realidad virtual, is a quiet, sun-drenched document of this refusal. At first glance, the title points to the figure lying in the foreground, face obscured by a headset, a body given over to a world we cannot see. But the longer you look, the more the title seems to infect the entire scene. The landscape itself feels like a virtual space, a constructed memory of a pastoral ideal. The landscape is rendered in soft, bleeding washes of colour that separate it into horizontal zones: a bruised blue sky, a hazy green pasture populated by what might be bison or cattle, a shore, and a stretch of warm, ochre ground. The setting is not a specific place. The painting shows the idea of a place, an archetype assembled from a thousand other pictures of a peaceful countryside.

The painting finds its strange power by staging a competition between two kinds of unreality. The figures in the foreground are not so much ignoring a beautiful landscape as inhabiting one virtual space while physically located in another. They are ghosts in two machines at once. Moreno Sánchez replaces the classical dream of Arcadia, seen in pastoral fantasies like Titian's Concert champêtre, with a contemporary version. Where Titian’s figures were bound by a shared, if mysterious, mythological mood, the figures here are islands. The man hunched over his phone and the body lost in its headset are communing not with nature or each other, but with a different signal. Their separation from their surroundings is announced by the colour used to paint them: they are stained in hot, unnatural pinks and magentas, the palette of a screen’s glare, not of sunlight on skin.

This concern with the screen as a disruptive presence, a source of both connection and profound absence, has run through his work for over a decade. The lone figure consumed by the glow of a laptop in Chatting (2010) is an ancestor to the figures here. So are the dead televisions lying like beached whales in the fractured landscape of Pantallas (2010). With Realidad virtual, painted fifteen years later, the technology is no longer just a feature in the room but a condition of being in the world itself, as portable and immediate as the grass the figures sit on. Even the act of watching has become a theme, as seen in the earlier Mirando reels (2015).

The watercolor itself, with its fugitive, pooling colours, works against the crisp, defined worlds offered by the digital devices. The paint admits its own uncertainty. What those animal forms are in the middle distance, or who the small, sleeping baby-like figures are in the foreground, is left unresolved. They could be inhabitants of the pastoral, or avatars from the game, it is impossible to say for sure. The painting is perhaps less successful in its most literal element; the headset itself feels like a symbol almost too loud for the quiet ambiguity of the rest of the scene. The work is stronger where it is less direct, in the unsettling drift between the zones of reality.

The painting’s most poignant figure is the one whose title it borrows: the body in the headset, lying prone, face entirely gone. This posture suggests total surrender, a life temporarily unplugged from the world of blood and sun and offered up to another kind of input. The work is not a lament or a critique. The work is a landscape painting for a time when the landscape is just one of several realities available for consumption, and not always the most engaging one. It simply registers a new kind of human presence, which is a new kind of human absence.
