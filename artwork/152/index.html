Secuestro en la rave.

2024, Oil on canvas, 130 x 130 cm.

The Party at the End of the World

The year 2024 marks a century since André Breton published the Manifesto of Surrealism, giving a name to a revolution already in motion. The Surrealists sought a truth deeper than the visible, a reality unlocked by plumbing the depths of the psyche through dreams, chance, and automatic writing. They followed the "royal road to the unconscious," as Freud had called it, believing that the marvelous, the convulsive, and the strange lay waiting in the unlit rooms of the human mind. A hundred years on, in a world saturated with images, a painter like Juanma Moreno Sánchez finds the surreal by a different road. He finds it not in the dreaming mind, but in the algorithmic hallucinations of a machine.

The painting's title is Secuestro en la rave (Kidnapping at the rave). It is a violent phrase for a scene that might otherwise be a party.

The first thing we see, or the first person, is the boy. He sits apart on the left, his face a study in classical painterly attention. The light catches the soft curve of his cheek, the damp hair plastered to his forehead, the astonishment in his wide eyes and open mouth. His body is rendered with a tenderness that makes his alarm feel real and urgent. He is our witness. He is the human anchor in a scene that is coming undone. We cannot know what horror, or what wonder, has seized his attention, only that he is looking out of the painting, past us, at the source of the disturbance. His surprise is our own. In this, he feels like a direct descendant of the figures in Édouard Manet’s Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe, who break the fiction of the pastoral scene by acknowledging the viewer’s gaze. Here, the boy does not see us; he sees what is coming for us.

He is watching the kidnapping. But who is being taken? At the centre of the canvas is a chaotic assembly, a knot of figures that refuse to resolve into individual people. Where their heads should be, there are faceted, crystalline shapes, like gems or hoods made of solid light, in shades of emerald and ruby. One figure, a cascade of blonde hair, is flanked by these strange beings. Lower down, a fleshy, larval form collapses into a heap of painterly matter, a body without the integrity of a body. This is the artist’s signature territory, the imagery he coaxes from generative artificial intelligence, and it is the subject of his 2024 exhibition El Valle Inquietante at Galería Zunino in Seville. These are not people at a rave; they are apparitions from the latent space, digital ghosts given the weight and smell of oil paint. They are a collective, but an illegible one, their gathering less a party than a contagion. They feel like something from a dream, but it is not a human dream. This is what the forest looks like when the machines are sleeping in it, as in the unsettling companion piece from the same year, Catástrofe en el bosque.

And then there is the yellow paint. At the bottom of the work, a stroke of brilliant, industrial yellow cuts across the scene, its drips running down over the dark, churned-up forest floor. It is clearly spray paint. It is not a color that belongs in a forest, or at a party. It is a mark made on the painting’s surface, an act of vandalism against its own illusion. This slash of artificial color feels like a glitch, a tear in the fabric of the image itself. The gesture runs a risk, of course. The spray-painted slash as a sign for the digital could become an easy shorthand, a graphic tic. But here it succeeds because of its violence. It feels like an artifact from a different reality that has bled through. The writer and artist Hito Steyerl, in her essay "In Defense of the Poor Image," describes the degraded, compressed, endlessly copied JPEGs and AVIs that populate our screens as "the trash that washes up on the digital economies’ shores." That slash of yellow is one of those ghosts. It is a piece of digital debris that has washed up here, in a forest clearing, inside a painting.

Perhaps the title is not about the boy at all. Perhaps no single person is being abducted. The title might name the event itself. This is the kidnapping of the rave, the abduction of the very idea of a human gathering. It is the moment when the familiar ritual of bodies coming together in music and ecstasy is invaded and replaced by something else, something alien. The painting leaves us with the boy’s shocked face, a witness to the moment his world became populated by beautiful, terrifying things he cannot name, his party turning into a ceremony for a future he does not understand.
