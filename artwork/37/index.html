Blessed.

2017, Watercolor on paper, 21 x 29 cm.

The Unread Blessing

To put on a costume is to propose a new kind of body, an act distinct from simply hiding who you are. A costume is an argument worn as a skin. It allows a figure to be two things at once, or perhaps three, held in a state of fragile, temporary assembly. The body becomes a collage of signs, and its meaning, if it has one, lies in the friction between them. So it is with the figure that kneels in the dark theater of this small watercolor, a performance for an audience of one, the painter himself.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Blessed gives us a body made entirely of costume. A figure with human limbs is sheathed in the fluffy pink wool of a lamb, the black stockings and gloves of a fetish club, the floppy ears of a cartoon rabbit, and the impassive, doll-like face of a mask. Each element is an echo from a different cultural world, and none of them agree. The work is a study in incongruity, a creature of pure performance, crawling out of an inky wash of darkness into a shallow pool of blue light. This fascination with the figure as a performance of a role, rather than a portrait of a person, appears elsewhere in Moreno Sánchez's work, from the public spectacle of El artista excéntrico to the unsettling ritual of Easter bunny, another human-animal hybrid from the same year.

This watercolor from 2017 is a crucial marker. The strange, composite bodies that would later populate his canvases, often sparked by the suggestions of generative algorithms, were first being pieced together by hand. The uncanny here is not an algorithmic accident but a human invention, assembled from the cultural debris of innocence and sexuality. A later work like Monstruo del espacio latente (I) shows a being whose form is genuinely alien, a product of the machine’s non-human imagination. Blessed is its ancestor, but its components are all too familiar. It demonstrates that the impulse to create these hybrid beings, to explore the valley between the human and the not-quite, predates the tool. The interest was always in the monster, not the machine that might one day help to summon it.

The problem of a hybrid body that others fail to correctly identify is an old one in Spanish art. It calls to mind a print from Goya’s Los Caprichos, Plate 7: Ni asi la distingue ("Even thus he cannot make her out"). In it, a preening man uses a monocle to inspect a woman, his optical tool serving only to confirm his own delusions. He cannot see her for what she is. Goya’s print was a clear social satire, a critique of the follies he saw around him. Moreno Sánchez’s watercolor, by contrast, offers no such stable ground for critique. It is not clear if this costumed figure is a victim, a performer in control, or something else entirely. The ambiguity is the point.

And then there is the title. Blessed. One word, dropped onto the scene like a stone into water. The title forces a spiritual reading that the image itself seems to reject at every turn. It wrenches this creature out of the world of private desire and into the language of grace. I cannot know if the title is an act of supreme irony, a private joke, or a moment of genuine, startling sincerity. This refusal to resolve is where the painting’s true strength lies. The word saves the image from being merely an illustration of a subculture, making it stranger and more potent. It denies us the comfort of classification.

The painting’s quietest success is its own vulnerability. The watercolor is thin, the pink wash bleeds unevenly into the paper, and the initial pencil drawing remains visible in the loops of the fleece, a ghost of the first thought. This evidence remains of a hand moving across paper, trying to fix a form that seems to want to dissolve. The blessing might instead be for the irresolvable question the creature poses, an image that remains, finally, unread.
