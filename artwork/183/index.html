Un altar antiguo.

2025, Oil on canvas, 27 x 27 cm.

The Altar of the Instant

To paint an act of devotion now is to work with the wreckage of a language. The old machinery of sacred art, with its shared symbols and legible miracles, has been dismantled. A painter who still wants to show a body reaching for something beyond itself can no longer borrow the light of heaven; it has to be generated from within the scene, often from the unstable material of the human form itself. The problem is what to do when that form is no longer solid, when the bodies themselves are as fleeting as the faith they might once have represented.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Un altar antiguo walks directly into this predicament. The title (An ancient altar) makes a promise of permanence, of stone and ritual carved to outlast generations. The canvas delivers the opposite. In a bright, almost bucolic landscape of green fields and blooming roses, a precarious tower of figures struggles to hold its shape. The structure is an altar of flesh, and failing flesh at that. The three bodies, layered like ghostly afterimages, seem to melt into one another, their forms so unstable it is difficult to tell where one ends and the next begins. The entire structure feels temporary, an offering built for the instant alone.

This vertical arrangement of bodies has two powerful but contradictory ancestors in Spanish art. The first is the folk tradition of the castells, the human towers of Catalonia built through immense collective strength, trust, and precision. Moreno Sánchez’s structure is a private, spectral inversion of that public ritual; where a castell is a monument to community, this is a fragile column of solitude. Its other forebear is from a far darker place: the desperate, winged figures in Goya’s etching Modo de volar, whose attempts at flight are an exercise in heroic, probably fatal, delusion. Moreno Sánchez's painting occupies the space between the celebrated ritual and the private madness, a gesture of ascent stripped of its audience and its certainty.

The dissolving quality of these figures is a hallmark of the painter’s recent work, which often finds its source material in the impossible anatomies dreamed up by generative algorithms, as in the monstrously serene Ángel III. Yet the effect here feels less like a machine’s logic and more like a memory, or a flaw in a photographic print. It connects to an older preoccupation, visible in a work like Stolen pic II from more than a decade earlier, with translating the glitches and blurs of a fleeting digital image into the slow, deliberate language of oil. The landscape itself, with its high-keyed, indifferent green, is a zone of strange tranquility, a paradise oblivious to the human drama it contains, recalling the unsettling quiet of Catástrofe en el bosque. The painting's style is a long way from the more straightforwardly observed world of an early painting like Paisaje nostálgico.

For all its energy, the painting courts a certain schematic simplicity. The serene landscape set against the struggling figures, the rose bush blooming at the edge of the frame; these are sharp, almost symbolic contrasts. The work’s success hangs on whether this legibility feels like a deliberate return to archetypes or a solution taken too easily. I find myself unable to decide. What prevents the canvas from collapsing into a mere diagram is its palpable speed and its small size. The thick, hasty application of paint feels honest about the vision’s ephemerality. The result is a thought captured before it disappeared, its execution as precarious as the structure it depicts.

The final irony is in the title’s claim to be antiguo. Nothing here is ancient except the ambition to raise something up, to make a gesture toward the sky. The figures are not celebrating; they are straining. The uppermost body, held aloft like a saint’s relic, is the most transparent of all, its arm lifted in a gesture that could be a wave, a plea, or a final dissolution. The painting does not show us the god this altar was built for. It shows us only the physical cost of the attempt, the sheer effort of holding a shape, for one more second, against the pull of an empty blue.
