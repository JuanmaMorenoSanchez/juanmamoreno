Pantallas.

2010, Oil on canvas, 185 x 240 cm.

The Shore of the After-Image

To paint a landscape has always been to propose a single, coherent world. The frame of the canvas acts as a window, and through that window we are offered a unified place, seen from one perspective, at one moment in time. The very first decision Juanma Moreno Sánchez makes in Pantallas is to break that promise. By splitting the canvas into two separate panels, he announces that this will not be a picture of a single world, but of a world already in pieces, a place that no longer adds up.

The thin brass-colored line that divides the painting is not a subtle interruption; it is an architectural fact, a pillar that stands between our eyes and the scene. It forces us to read the landscape as two distinct thoughts. On the left, a church; on the right, a factory. This is a classical, almost tired, contrast between faith and industry, the old Europe and the new. But the painting sets up this opposition only to show how little it matters to the people within the frame. The figures lounging on the riverbank, their backs to the view, are indifferent to the grand symbolic battle being waged on the opposite shore. They have other concerns, or perhaps none at all. Their quiet lethargy feels like a very modern kind of exhaustion.

This painting’s title means "Screens," and they are the most alien objects here. Lying on the muddy bank and half-sunk in the water, they are not the glowing portals to other worlds we see in later paintings like "Chatting" or the slick surface of "Reflejo de luz de iPad". These are the heavy, obsolete television sets of a recently bygone era, the cathode-ray tubes now turned into garbage. They are beached things, fossils of a technology that has already washed ashore and died. Their blank faces reflect nothing. They are the mute, heavy residue of our forward momentum, a new kind of industrial waste. The dog sniffing at one seems to give it more attention than any of the humans do.

The figures themselves are a catalogue of disconnected states: walking, lounging, embracing, looking away.

There is a slight weakness in the rendering of these people; they feel less like individuals and more like types, placeholders for postures of leisure. But this may be a deliberate choice. It prevents us from reading the scene as a story about specific relationships and instead forces our attention onto the atmosphere, the strange collage of elements. It finds a distant, inverted echo in the work of an artist like George Bellows, whose 1906 painting River Rats shows urchins on New York’s banks teeming with a scrappy, unified energy. The figures in Pantallas, by contrast, possess no such shared vitality. They share a space, but not a moment. They are together, but they are not with each other.

I cannot be certain if the effect is deliberate, but the single tree on the left panel seems to be falling apart in a distinctly digital way. Its leaves dissolve upwards into a scatter of marks, a spray of noise that resembles a pixelated glitch. In an oil painting from 2010, this is an uncanny sight, as if the reality of the scene itself is degrading. The world here is not merely broken into two by the diptych’s structure; it is fraying at the edges, its own data becoming corrupt. This sense of one reality intruding on another is a recurring current in his work from this period, from the impossible zoology of "A friend" to the ambiguous apocalypse of "El fin del mundo".

One boy, his back to us, walks out of the frame toward our own space. He is the painting’s only point of clear momentum, a figure abandoning this fractured pastoral. His stripes are the loudest pattern in a muted landscape. Is he walking away in protest, or is he simply bored? Like the other strollers in paintings such as "Transeúntes", his purpose is a mystery. He leaves the scene unresolved, trailing its quiet contradictions behind him.
