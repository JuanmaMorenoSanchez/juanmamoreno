Veinteañeros en la Feria de San Mateo.

2010, Oil on canvas, 162 x 114 cm.

A Terrible Stillness

A painter who takes on the modern crowd walks into a trap set more than a century ago. In 1888, the Belgian artist James Ensor painted Christ’s Entry into Brussels in 1889, and in doing so, he gave us the definitive image of the crowd as a grotesque mob. His Brussels is a parade of leering masks, a churning, chaotic sea of frauds and clowns who completely ignore the small figure of Christ in their midst. It is a painting about the failure of community, a vision of society as a collection of monstrous, alienated individuals. Any painting of a party made since has to reckon with Ensor’s diagnosis: that to be together in number is not to be together in spirit.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s large 2010 canvas knows this history. The title, Veinteañeros en la Feria de San Mateo, promises a specific scene of collective life: twenty-somethings at a traditional Spanish fair. It suggests noise, music, and shared release. The painting itself, however, is a suffocatingly quiet picture of that noise. A group of young people are pressed into a dark, ambiguous space, a pile-up of bodies so dense it feels airless. They are at the same party, but they are not in the same world. One woman launches a paper airplane into a wall of flesh. Another, her face a mask of detached cool behind huge sunglasses, poses with a black-gloved hand. A man blows a whistle at no one. Each figure is busy performing their own private version of fun.

The result is a terrible stillness at the heart of the chaos.

The most eloquent objects here are the party props, the cheap talismans of forced joy. The funny glasses, the green hat, the lace gloves—they function like Ensor’s masks, hiding and distorting their wearers. The most haunting of these is the plastic bag held aloft by an unseen arm on the right. Inside is a doll, its smiling face looking out through the transparent film. I do not know if this is a prize won at the fair or a private, inscrutable joke. Its effect, however, is clear. The doll is a stand-in for a person, a suffocated effigy raised like a trophy. In a painting full of living people trying and failing to connect, this dead thing becomes the starkest, most cynical symbol of the entire affair.

Made in 2010, this painting is a grand, early thesis statement for themes that Moreno Sánchez would continue to refine for the next decade. The profound alienation within a social gathering, staged here with the full cast of a history painting, gets distilled into more focused scenes of digital-age isolation in works like Otro puto selfie (2014) and the multipaneled Pantallas (2010). Compared to the slick, otherworldly horrors of a more recent canvas like Festival de influencers (2025), which feels born of a machine’s fever dream, this painting is a work of analogue observation. It is a document of the messy, physical world of bodies in a room, before the uncanny valley opened up to swallow the party whole.

The painting’s ambition is also its greatest risk. The composition is a claustrophobic crush that threatens to become illegible, a heap of anecdotes rather than a single, coherent statement. Yet this is a risk the artist takes in oil, a slow and deliberate medium. The paint is solid and weighty, treating this scene of fleeting, youthful disconnection with a seriousness that borders on the monumental. The cheap sunglasses and plastic toys are rendered with the same gravity as the flesh and bone. The painting insists that this mess matters.

In the bottom left corner, a woman with a sharp haircut and wide, startled eyes sips her drink through a straw. Of all the figures locked in their own performance, she is the one who seems to be looking out, as if she has just noticed the viewer, or perhaps just noticed the deep strangeness of the room she is in. Her gaze does not explain anything. It simply confirms that something is deeply wrong here, and leaves us inside the question.
