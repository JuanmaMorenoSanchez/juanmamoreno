Ángel II.

2024, Oil on wood, 30 x 30 cm.

A Figure Among Things

Every painting is a record of a body, but sometimes the body that matters most is the body of the paint itself. A painter can render flesh so it feels warm to the touch, or they can allow the pigment to curdle, to assert its own material fact, to remind you that you are looking at a worked surface, not a window. Juanma Moreno Sánchez, in his recent work, has been fascinated by this choice. In Ángel II, a small, quiet canvas from 2024, he stages a confrontation between these different states of matter, and the nominal subject, a kneeling nude, becomes the arena for their contest.

The figure is rendered with a familiar, classical warmth. The light models the skin, finding the weight in a thigh and the sharp plane of a collarbone. The effect is of a body of flesh, solid and present. But this body hosts two invasions. The first is a patch of thick, turquoise pigment on the chest, a textural anomaly that refuses to be read as clothing or flesh. This patch is not drapery but an island of pure paint, its churned surface announcing its otherness. Here, the painting’s material breaks through its own illusion. This kind of anatomical strangeness is a feature of his latest work, as in the glorious excess of limbs in Ángel III, but here the disturbance is not in the form but in the substance.

The second invasion is the vessel in the foreground. The vessel is a simple, grey, almost featureless pot, cool, solid, and without texture. The pot stands as the opposite of the turbulent blue pigment. Where the blue is all raw material, the pot is all pure form. It has the chill of a plaster model or a digital rendering, a stock object from the studio dropped into this warm, breathing world. It brings to mind the quiet dignity afforded to household objects by the eighteenth-century French painter Anne Vallayer-Coster, who could make a simple terrine the solemn anchor of a composition. Moreno Sánchez treats this pot with a similar gravity, but by placing it beside a living figure, he creates a hierarchy of things, not of beings. The nude, the pot, and the patch of paint are all given equal standing as painterly events.

This is not a sudden turn. His direct engagement with the genre is clear in an austere, frontal canvas from the same year, simply titled Bodegón (Still Life). But seeing that same thinking applied to the human figure marks a change. A painting like Carne from 2012 presented the nude as an uncomplicated site of sensual presence. Now, the body has become a more complex surface, a place where different modes of representation can meet and argue. The figure becomes a still life that happens to be alive.

There is a risk in this assembly of parts. The painting’s composure feels provisional, as if these three warring elements, flesh, form, and pigment, are held in a truce that might not last. I cannot decide if the sheer simplicity of the grey vessel is a masterstroke of restraint or an admission of an unresolved question, a placeholder for an idea not yet fully formed. Its blankness is both its strength and its weakness. This blankness becomes a point of stillness in a complex field, and also a void.

The painting’s title, Ángel II, offers no easy key. Perhaps the messenger here is not the figure, but the unsettling quiet that hangs over this collection of things. The work’s most arresting passage might be its smallest: the gentle curve of the figure’s knee as it rests against the cold, unyielding purple shape on which she kneels. Here, in this junction of the living and the abstract, the painting’s argument is made. This junction becomes a moment of impossible, material tenderness, a body finding its place among the other objects of the world.
