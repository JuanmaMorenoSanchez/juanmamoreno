Noche caótica.

2018, Watercolor on paper, 21 x 29 cm.

The Shape of a Secret

To paint a secret is to solve a problem of geometry. The subject is not the information being withheld, but the physical shape of the withholding: the angle of a head, the space between two bodies, the precise boundary where one person’s breath meets another’s skin. A painter who can render that boundary has said enough. The secret itself is irrelevant. They have painted the container, and the container is the thing that holds us. The force of the work lies in what the painter chooses to show of that container and, more importantly, what is left opaque.

In Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Noche caótica (Chaotic Night), the most opaque object is a block of pure, startling blue. A visor, pulled low, has completely erased a face, replacing it with a smooth, synthetic, and featureless curve. The visor is not a mask that suggests a hidden identity; it is a void. The shape is a cipher at the heart of an intimate scene, a moment of quiet surrender where one figure leans into the warmth of another. The painting stages an act of trust directed towards a blank. This gesture recurs in his work, this use of a modern object to complicate a timeless human act, as with the intrusive red snorkel that disrupts the peace in Verano luminoso VI from 2012. Here, the object is more absolute. It anticipates the inhuman forms, born from algorithms, that would come to populate his canvases years later, as in the calm, unsettling body of Ángel III. In this 2018 watercolor, although the machine has not yet arrived, its shadow is cast by a piece of plastic.

The conflict between the hidden and the exposed finds a historical echo in the woodcuts of the Swiss painter Félix Vallotton. In a print like The Lie (1897), from his Intimités series, a figure whispers into another’s ear, their forms merging into a single dark mass from which only a few facial features escape. Vallotton used the harsh, graphic economy of the woodcut to charge domestic scenes with a sense of profound and often menacing secrecy. Moreno Sánchez achieves a similar effect through a collision of materials. The visor is a flat, hard-edged shape of watercolor, while the second figure is all tender, vulnerable flesh, her skin rendered in a nervous web of pink and red colored pencil. A thin purple line, a signature that runs through much of his work from this period, traces the contours of both, a fragile attempt to hold two different visual realities together in one frame.

The painting’s success depends on whether these two realities can coexist without cancelling each other out. The risk is that the scene splits, becoming a mere diagram of opposition: plastic versus skin, hidden versus revealed. Moreno Sánchez avoids this by plunging both figures into a deep, indeterminate darkness, a liquid night that threatens to dissolve their edges. This work feels like the quiet aftermath of the nocturnal scenes he painted in the same year, like the scattered gatherings in the Fiesta en el parking series. The chaos of the title has already happened. This is what is left: a fragile structure of support, built in the dark.

I cannot know the nature of the relationship between these two figures, nor what precise emotion is meant by the upward tilt of a chin or the slight tension in a hand. To even ask feels like a category error. The painting is less concerned with psychology than with a physics of closeness. The painting is a study in textures: the absorbent texture of paper, the grainy texture of pencil on that paper, the imagined texture of skin, and the hard, reflective non-texture of plastic. The work is about how these different surfaces receive the light, and how one of them refuses to receive it at all.

Against the cool blues and greens that dominate the frame, there is only one point of warmth. A single cigarette, held between two fingers, glows with a tiny ember. It is a small, organic light source, a speck of slow combustion in a scene defined by the cold, synthetic presence of the visor. The cigarette is an object of finite life, a timer counting down. The painting captures the moment just before it burns out, leaving the figures to a darkness that feels both intimate and absolute.
