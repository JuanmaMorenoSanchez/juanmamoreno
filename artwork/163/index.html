Fotógrafa.

2010, Watercolor on paper, 45,5 x 30,5 cm.

To paint a person taking a photograph is to create a strangely doubled image. One machine for making pictures, the hand-guided brush, depicts another, the mechanical eye of the camera. The painter is not showing us the world, but showing us someone else in the act of capturing it. The subject is no longer the landscape, or the person, but the frame itself, the decision to select a piece of the world and declare it an image. This act of framing is where the meaning lives, and it is an act that Juanma Moreno Sánchez returned to repeatedly in the years around 2010, in works like the furtive "Stolen pic" series or the unsettling "Voyeur".

Fotógrafa places us behind the photographer. We see what she sees, but only crudely. A mass of turbulent green trees, a lawn, and something else at the edge of the woods. A low fence, painted a stubborn, artificial purple, and behind it, the dark, hunched shapes of what can only be tombstones. She is taking pictures in a cemetery. Her camera, a dense black machine, hides her face, replacing her expression with the professional apparatus of her role. The machine is the face. The title defines her by her function, rather than as a person who might be grieving or remembering. She is the photographer.

What is she doing?

The picture offers no story. Is she a tourist, collecting macabre sights? An artist, documenting the landscape of memory? A journalist on assignment? The painting withholds the narrative and gives us only the posture. A woman in a bright red shirt stands on the vivid green grass of the living world, pointing a machine for freezing time at a place that marks its absolute end. The solution of placing a red figure against a green ground is one of painting’s oldest tricks for getting attention, a debt to color theory worn so openly it feels less like a reference and more like a shrug. It works, but its obviousness feels like a deflection, turning our eyes from the real question.

This scene is a modern revision of a very old painterly problem, one that Nicolas Poussin captured in his masterwork, Et in Arcadia ego. In that quiet, classical landscape, a group of shepherds has discovered a tomb and are puzzling out its inscription. They are figures of innocence confronting the fact of mortality, deciphering a message sent from the past. Even in paradise, the inscription tells them, death is here. Moreno Sánchez’s photographer is no innocent. She stands in for us. She is not there to decipher a message from the dead but to make her own, to take something away. The camera is a promise of preservation, a tool for arresting decay, and she is deploying it at the one site that proves the futility of that entire project. The tension in the painting comes from this contradiction: the vital, momentary act of capturing an image set against the silent, permanent fact of the grave.

The choice of watercolor for this subject is a curious one. Watercolor is the medium of the fleeting, the provisional, the impression caught in a wash before it evaporates. Here, it is used to depict a scene centred on a machine built for permanence. I do not know if this was a deliberate irony or simply the material at hand for a study, but the result is a powerful instability. The forest behind the photographer bleeds and dissolves, a restless, almost liquid backdrop. The ground beneath her feet is a stain of vibrant color. The image feels as if it might collapse back into water and pigment at any moment, much like the moments the photographer herself is trying to fix. This sense of the provisional, of a world caught in passing, runs through many of his watercolors from this time, like the quickly-glimpsed figures in "Domingo por la tarde en el parque".

Look at the camera. Moreno Sánchez has rendered it with more solidity than anything else in the painting. And at its heart is the lens: a perfect, flat, black circle. It is a void. That void is the destination for all the light in this green and pleasant place. The camera’s lens does not reflect the world, as a painter’s eye might; it simply absorbs it. It is an information-gathering machine whose aperture gives nothing back. The aperture is the hole in the center of the picture, promising to save a memory while looking, to us, like a small piece of oblivion.
