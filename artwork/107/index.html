Verano sombrío I.

2016, Watercolor on paper, 22 x 16 cm.

The Corrosion of the Light

A painter can return to a subject a hundred times and find it unchanged. Or they can return once and find that the world has soured. In 2012, Juanma Moreno Sánchez made a series of small, joyous watercolors he called Verano luminoso (“Luminous Summer”). They are what they claim to be: quick, bright, near-abstract studies of light and water, so small and confident they feel like captured photons. Four years later, in 2016, he returned to the theme. This new painting is titled Verano sombrío I, and the shift in that one word is everything. The somberness is not a feeling imposed on the scene. This somberness is a chemical property of the painting itself, a failure in the light that was once the work’s entire reason for being.

The subject is a body made of trapped sunlight. The figure’s skin is a hot, saturated gold, the colour of a summer that should be blissful. But this body is held captive by its environment. The blue that surrounds it is not the gentle, varied blue of a sky or a sea. This blue is a flat, airless, and deeply artificial field, a substance that seems to have more in common with a chemical bath or the cold light of a screen than with any natural atmosphere. The blue presses in on the figure, containing her, its flat intensity making the warmth of her skin seem not like a pleasure but a vulnerability, a fever in a cold room. The light is still present, but it no longer illuminates. It only exposes.

This active corrosion is most obvious where the body should be most grounded. At the bottom of the paper, the figure’s legs and feet simply cease to be solid. The watercolour pigment bleeds out, dissolving the form into a pooling stain, a ghost of a footprint that melts into the raw, unpainted surface. This disintegration is not an artist’s sketch, an unfinished thought. The disintegration is the painting’s most deliberate act. This act of dissolution is a declaration that this figure has no ground to stand on, that the world she occupies is literally falling away beneath her. The somberness of this summer is an acid, eating away at the body from the ground up, even as her torso remains, for a moment, whole. The painting is a portrait of a person coming undone in place.

In this, Moreno Sánchez finds an unlikely ancestor in the late works of Auguste Rodin. The sculptor’s late watercolour drawings, like his Nude with Draperies, often let the medium have its own way. Rodin would draw a quick, sure line in graphite and then apply washes of colour that bled past the body’s edges, creating figures that seem to be condensing out of or dissolving into pure energy. For Rodin, this was a way to capture movement and life. For Moreno Sánchez, working a century later in a much colder light, this same technique of dissolution speaks of precarity. This technique is the language of a body losing its integrity, its form surrendering to the medium that depicts it. A body, like a photograph, can fade.

The painting’s success depends on this material failure. The pose itself, cropped from behind, the leopard-print bikini, the hand resting on the hip, all court the cliché of a thousand disposable holiday snapshots. I cannot be sure what the hand’s gesture means; it seems caught between adjustment and assertion, too ambiguous to read as a single clear signal. The work takes a huge risk in adopting the grammar of a generic, consumable image. The work is saved, just, by its own material distress. The hostile blue, the dissolving feet, the pencil lines that score the surface like scars, are all evidence of the painting fighting back against its photographic source.

This is the first somber summer, the start of a new line of inquiry that would continue with works like Verano sombrío II. The painting is an answer to the easy joy of the Verano luminoso series. The light has not simply dimmed. It has turned against the figure it describes, and the result is a beautiful, quiet painting about the process of coming apart.
