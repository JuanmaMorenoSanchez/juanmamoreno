Siesta en el brasero.

2019, Oil on canvas, 41 x 24 cm.

The Hum of the Machine

To paint sleep has always been to paint a private kingdom. The sleeping body is a closed circuit, a fortress of flesh sealed against the world, and the painting that shows it has traditionally honoured that privacy. The work becomes a study in quietness, a portrait of a temporary peace. But peace is a fragile state, and the painter’s problem today is that the world has found new ways to get in. The kingdom is no longer sealed. The kingdom is permeated by signals, buzzing with a low, electronic hum that continues even after the eyes are closed.

Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Siesta en el brasero is a painting about a sleep that has been infiltrated. The title promises a specific, almost archaic form of Spanish domestic warmth, the collective comfort found around a table heated from below. The image, however, delivers a contradiction. We see a man in a state of deep surrender, but he seems to be outdoors, under a clear sky and beside a dark mass of foliage. The communal, interior warmth of the brasero has been replaced by the solitary heat of the sun. The setting is already unstable before the real intruder makes itself known.

That intruder is a colour: a sour, electric green that traces the edges of the man’s body and the patterns on his pillow. The electric green is not a light source within the scene. The colour is a foreign substance, a digital artifact laid over the surface of the painting. It has the quality of a video glitch, a data corruption, a signal leaking from the small, screen-like rectangle visible at the bottom of the canvas. The line is the hum made visible. It crawls over the sleeper’s face, a parasitic line of code that shows his rest is not entirely his own. This work, from 2019, thus becomes a hinge in Moreno Sánchez's practice. It looks back to the fraught social spaces of a painting like Pantallas (2010), but the digital interference here is more intimate, a premonition of the fully synthetic bodies that would emerge a year later in works like Monstruo del espacio latente (I).

An older painting of a sleeper being visited by an external force had a clearer grammar. In Giotto’s fresco The Dream of Joachim, from the Scrovegni Chapel, an angel descends to deliver a divine message to the sleeping man. The angel is a messenger from another world, breaking into the shepherd’s reality with a purpose. The lurid green line in Moreno Sánchez’s painting is the modern angel, but it is an angel with no message. This angel is pure signal, an impersonal transmission from the machine world that now occupies the space a sacred vision once did. The sleeper is still being visited, but the visitor is a ghost without a story, an interference that offers no revelation, only its own persistent, meaningless presence.

The handling of the green itself is telling. The green is applied with a flatness that seems to refuse the painterly world around it. One might argue the mark is too literal, a simple signifier for "digital" that risks becoming a cliché. But its very crudeness may be the point. It does not blend; it sits on top of the image, an uninvited guest who refuses to learn the local customs. The painting finds its strange, melancholic power in this failed integration. I cannot be certain if we are seeing a man sleeping in a field or a bedroom where the wall holds a picture of a landscape. The space is as ambiguous as the sleep. What is certain is that the rest is incomplete. A body is here, but its peace is conditional, subject to the vibrations of a device lying just out of sight, humming.
