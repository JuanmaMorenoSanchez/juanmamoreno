El punk.

2010, Watercolor on paper, 40,5 x 30,5 cm.

The Anarchist's Guide to Birdsong

A title is a frame. It tells you what to see, what to look for, what kind of story you are about to be told. So when a watercolor from 2010 names its protagonist El punk, it sets up an expectation of noise, of negation, of a certain kind of uniform. The man at the centre of Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s painting wears a black t-shirt with what looks like a logo of rebellion, and he holds a beer bottle. The promise of the title is, for a moment, kept. But a title can also be a misdirection, and a punk can be more than one kind of trouble. The man's attention is not on us, or on any visible enemy. His mouth is open, and he is looking at a small brown bird.

The painting is a catalogue of things that do not belong together, a scene built from the friction of mismatched parts. A heavy, civic lamppost stands sentry over an ornate, blue-velvet dining chair that has somehow migrated outdoors. The man, who we are told is a punk, seems to be mid-song, or mid-sentence, addressed to a bird that has perched on this incongruous piece of furniture. In the background, a woman is entirely sealed in her own world, separate from this strange communion. The scene refuses to resolve into a coherent narrative. The punk gesture, it turns out, is found in the painting's own method: an anarchic assembly of elements that refuse to obey a single logic. The work is less a portrait and more a quiet, inexplicable event.

This act of addressing an animal finds a distant, inverted ancestor in the Dutch Golden Age. Carel Fabritius’s famous panel, The Goldfinch, is a portrait of a bird as a possession, a creature defined by its chain and its perch, a piece of living decoration. Moreno Sánchez’s watercolor stages the opposite encounter. His bird is free, a wild thing that has wandered into the frame and become an audience of one. The man speaks to it, and in doing so, creates a relationship that is entirely absurd and entirely sincere. This fascination with impossible friendships appears in other early works, like the quiet compact between a girl and a giant anteater in another 2010 watercolor, "A friend". What they share is a refusal of plausible company, a turn towards a private logic.

Made long before the algorithmically generated bodies of his recent work, this painting reveals that Moreno Sánchez's interest in the uncanny has deep roots in simple observation. The strangeness here is social and situational, rather than monstrous or technological. The profound disconnections between figures sharing a space would become a central theme, visible in the partitioned worlds of a work like "Voyeur" or the anonymous drift of "Transeúntes". In El punk, the separation is acute. The woman in the background is so detached, rendered with such a lack of detail, that she almost feels like a memory from a different painting entirely.

This looseness is both a strength and a risk. The work’s charm lies in its very lack of cohesion, its refusal to pull its disparate elements into a tight, formal structure. But it also leaves the composition feeling precarious, as if the whole scene might dissolve back into its constituent parts: a man, a bird, a chair, a woman, a pole. Whether this fragility is a deliberate part of the painting’s meaning or simply a consequence of its quick, provisional medium is a question the work leaves open. The fluid bleed of watercolor allows the sky and the ground to merge, further destabilizing a scene that was already standing on uncertain foundations.

The painting holds its breath in the space between the man’s open mouth and the listening bird. The scene creates the silence of a sound we cannot hear. It might be a shout, a curse, or a song. It could be a line from a punk anthem, repurposed as a lyric for a modern St. Francis. The painting does not say, and its refusal to clarify is where its truth lies. The true punk is the one who reserves his most urgent messages for the audience no one else can see.
