Autorretrato con Puri (IV).

2019, Oil on board, 40 x 30 cm.

How does an artist paint himself now? The heroic pose is impossible, a joke nobody believes. The forensic, mirror-study stare of the old masters feels like a historical costume. And the casual, phone-at-arm’s-length snapshot has been so thoroughly emptied of meaning that it has its own pejorative: the selfie. Juanma Moreno Sánchez titled a painting from 2014 with a sigh of exhaustion at the whole problem: Otro puto selfie ("Another fucking selfie"). This painting, five years later, is one of his answers. The painting is a self-portrait where the self has been almost entirely removed. The artist is present as a surface rather than a subject. He is the stage.

We look down from above, a vantage point that denies any possibility of a conventional portrait. The figure is reduced to a mound of dark hair and the anonymous bulk of a hoodie. That specific light blue is a wonderfully functional colour; it is the blue of workwear, of tarps, of things that are meant to be used, not admired. The human becomes a landscape, a warm hill for the real subject to occupy. The painter’s body is a piece of furniture for his cat. Portraiture becomes an act of submission, a depiction of the self defined entirely by its utility to another being. A painter’s identity lies in his willingness to be still enough for long enough, rather than in his face or his posture.

To serve as a pedestal for a cat.

The cat’s name is Puri, the title tells us. Her presence marks the fourth time she has appeared in a self-portrait, suggesting a relationship, an ongoing negotiation. This was a year of them. She sits enthroned, looking away, utterly indifferent to the structure supporting her. This has its own quiet art-historical lineage. The great Japanese printmaker Utagawa Kuniyoshi (1798–1861) was obsessed with cats, drawing them with a devotion that saw them frequently replace humans in his work, acting out historical scenes or parodies of daily life. In his studio, filled with cats, he saw them not just as pets but as characters with their own gravity and purpose. Moreno’s painting shares that sense of the cat as a sovereign creature whose world we are merely lucky to inhabit. Puri is not a symbol. She is the monarch.

The painting is made on board, and the oil is applied with a certain haste. You can feel the speed in the confident, unblended marks that define the cat’s patchy coat and the rough green of the background. The thinness of the paint allows the texture of the support to remain present. The work is not a grand statement. It feels more like a private note, a moment captured before it dissolved. This informality is the work’s strength, but it also carries a risk. A painting like this courts the accusation of being slight, a charming anecdote that does not ask for much of our time. Compared to the dense, unsettling narrative of a canvas from the same year like Crimen en el olivar, its stakes seem intentionally low. But its honesty is in that smallness. It sits closer to the quiet domesticity of paintings like Siesta en el brasero, where the subject is not an event but a state of being.

I cannot be entirely sure if the mood here is pure affection or if there is a sliver of weary comedy in it. Is this a portrait of love or of servitude? The artist is literally debasing himself, bowing his head so the animal can have a better view. And yet, there is a tenderness to the act. That submission is a choice. We are left looking at the precariousness of the whole arrangement. The cat’s paws are not dug in; she seems to be resting lightly, ready to depart at any moment. The series number, (IV), confirms this is not a singular pose but a recurring situation. It feels less like a formal portrait and more like evidence of a habit, another moment in the life of an artist and his cat, captured before one of them moved.
