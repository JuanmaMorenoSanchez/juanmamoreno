Dálmatas y vacas.

2012, Oil on canvas, 97 x 130 cm.

To wear a costume is to join a collective, to borrow an identity for a time. It is a uniform for a temporary state of being, and a painter who puts his figures in costume is usually making an argument about the group they have chosen to join. But sometimes the performance is over. Sometimes the players are left in their costumes with no script to follow, stranded in a world their borrowed identities were not made for. The uniform, meant to explain them, becomes the very source of their strangeness.

This is the gentle, baffling world of Juanma Moreno Sánchez’s Dálmatas y vacas. The painting offers a simple, almost comedic premise declared by its title: five people in dalmatian costumes sit watching cows in a field. It is a comedy of patterns, a confrontation between two kinds of spots. But the gag gives way to a feeling of profound and shared uselessness. Their backs are to us, which directs our gaze with theirs, but it also preserves their anonymity. They are a team, a single visual unit, complete with limp, fabric tails. The cluster of bright party balloons to their left suggests a celebration, but a celebration that seems to be happening elsewhere, or is already over. The figures are not festive; they are still, caught in a moment of quiet, collective waiting.

They are characters without a play. The truest ancestor for this dilemma lies in the melancholic theatre of Antoine Watteau rather than in surrealism. His paintings of commedia dell’arte troupes, like The Italian Comedians, often capture the actors during the awkward silence after the curtain has fallen, rather than in the midst of performance. They are still in character, still defined by their theatrical roles, but they seem uncertain of what to do next. Moreno Sánchez’s figures share this post-performance listlessness. They have dressed for an event, but now they are simply watching a pastoral scene that has no need for them, a gathering whose purpose has evaporated.

This use of costume as a source of unease is a recurring device in Moreno Sánchez's work, but it has not always been so gentle. In a painting from two years earlier, Hijos de puta (2010), a group of men in suits have their faces obscured by cheap animal masks. There, the disguise is an act of menace and brutal caricature, a portrait of power as a faceless, animal firm. Here, in 2012, the same gesture of putting on a costume produces the opposite effect: a fragile, aimless camaraderie emerges in place of threat. This canvas shares a year with the fractured narrative of El silencio (I), another scene of disconnected figures, but these five are at least disconnected together, sharing the same baffling predicament.

The whole construction risks being nothing more than its central, charmingly absurd idea. The paint is handled with a casualness that matches the makeshift quality of the costumes; the spots are applied with a looseness that feels deliberately un-doglike, more like a pattern on pyjamas. The painting is saved from being a simple one-liner by this commitment to its own placid mood. The work never explains why these people are here, or what they are waiting for. I suspect there is no story to find. The mystery is the point. The painting provides an image that, instead of an event, depicts the quiet, slightly sad, and deeply human moment that comes after the event has ended, when the only thing left to do is watch the world go on without you. A single red balloon floats free, a last thought escaping from the party.
